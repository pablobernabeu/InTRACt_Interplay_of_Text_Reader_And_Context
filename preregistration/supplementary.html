Supplementary material: Text properties and children’s reading pace in a multilingual storytelling app

S1. The theory object

The object is a single machine-readable file, theory/intract.theory.yaml, which the theoryforge package reads and checks (Bernabeu, 2026b). From this file, theory/make_nomological_net.R draws Figure 2 of the main text. Running theory/build_theory.R reproduces the checklist scores, the redundancy screen and the implications reported below, and theory/check_manuscript_claims.R prints the counts quoted here.

The object registers twelve constructs and eighteen propositions linking them, each with a mechanism and a direction, fifteen as directed edges of the causal graph and three as moderation claims. Six auxiliary assumptions are recorded as separate entries, five of them corresponding to questions put to the data controller (Table 2 of the main text). Seven of the object’s ten predictions are marked diagnostic against one of the six rival accounts it names.

The rigour checklist scores the object at 90.7 out of 100, and all twelve items pass. On falsifiability and precision, at least one prediction forbids an observation, all ten are interval predictions, and their mean severity exceeds the checklist’s threshold. For construct quality, every construct has a definition, an intended measurement and boundary conditions, no pair is flagged by the lexical redundancy screen (largest observed similarity 0.17), and the theory’s scope is stated. Every proposition states a mechanism, at least one relation is causal, and every prediction is reachable from a proposition in the graph. Auxiliary assumptions are fewer than propositions, and none was added defensively in response to a prediction. Finally, at least one prediction discriminates between registered alternatives, and the formal-model entry refers to the design specification used for the precision analysis.

Because the causal graph is acyclic, it yields thirty testable implications, of which the main text’s fourth outcome-neutral quality check tests three. The most consequential is that the three text properties should be mutually independent given the reading level and the generation route. A first version of the object, without the text-provenance construct, implied that they were independent unconditionally. That implication is false of any real corpus, because a text’s target reading level shifts all three property families at once. Adding text provenance as a common cause of all three, with an incoming path from the reader’s decoding automaticity, corrected the implication and produced the adjustment set the analysis uses.

The third tested implication is that the difficulty rating should carry no further information about reading level given reading time. The literature gives more specific grounds for expecting it to fail than for the other two. A reported feeling of difficulty is a metacognitive experience drawing on affective as well as cognitive input (Efklides, 2006). When 274 pupils of grades 7 to 9 solved mathematical problems, that feeling varied across the phases of a task and bore on performance only indirectly, through ideas about what the task required (Efklides et al., 1999). After a battery of cognitive tasks, 80 children aged 8 to 12, 38 with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, rated task difficulty and effort. The ratings were uncorrelated with each other and only weakly related to performance (Torres & Toplak, 2026). Among college readers, such judgements often rest on surface features of a text, which are poor cues to comprehension (Thiede et al., 2010). The main text’s fourth quality check states which omitted path a failure of this implication would identify and how that path would be reported.

S3. Language resources for the text features

We set out the reasons for the first rule under Materials and the limits of the uniform resources, then register the pipeline settings and diagnostics the fourth rule requires. Supplementary Table 3 lists each feature family’s resources for the ten languages of the structural sample, and the paragraphs after it treat Korean, the placement of every language not in the classification of orthographic depth used in the main text, and Greek. Supplementary Table 4 and the rules after it extend the same design to the app’s nine other languages, and the section ends with adult-rated affective values.

A multilingual design faces a measurement problem that a monolingual one does not. Concreteness scores from a 40,000-item English rating study, a 350,000-lemma German regression, a 1,400-item Spanish rating study and a 77-language embedding prediction are four different measurements under one label. If each language’s resource is chosen for its local quality, the method of measurement is perfectly confounded with language. Standardising within language removes differences in location and scale but not in construct. One coherent approach uses a single uniformly produced resource everywhere, accepting lower absolute quality for higher comparability. The other uses native norms and treats every cross-language comparison as descriptive. We choose uniformity, because language enters the confirmatory models as a grouping factor with varying slopes, and those slopes must mean the same thing in every language. The same reasoning keeps concreteness and age of acquisition, for which only native norms are admissible, out of the pooled lexical solution. The native-norm sensitivity analysis under Materials shows how much the choice of resource matters.

The rules under Materials exclude two widely used resources, each the obvious candidate for its family, so we record why. The wordfreq package is not the primary frequency source. Its data are a frozen snapshot of usage to about 2021, and its maintainer discontinued it in September 2024 to avoid ingesting post-2022 web text contaminated by generated language. Its Korean and Greek wordlists draw on fewer sources than the rest. The non-English editions of the NRC Valence, Arousal and Dominance lexicon are not used at all. They are 2022 machine translations of the English norms, not ratings collected in the target languages, and their licence is non-commercial.

The primary frequency resource, subs2vec, is built from subtitles and so indexes adult exposure. The register mismatch for developing readers is a limitation that no available uniform resource removes. A recent study found that frequency estimates from a large-language-model corpus prompted for child-directed text fitted young German readers better than child-book norms did (Schepens et al., 2025). Generated corpora, however, are neither frozen nor documented in the way a subtitle pipeline is, and the same study notes that they show atypical linguistic patterns. We therefore keep subs2vec as the primary resource. Wherever a language has a native child-register norm, we will report the primary frequency estimate’s correlation with that norm as a measure of its register mismatch. The choice of frequency database also matters for reading-time fit. A pooled analysis of five self-paced reading studies in German found that a written-corpus database fitted sentential reading times best, ahead of subtitle frequencies, and that the ranking of databases differed by participant population (Haeuser & Kray, 2025). That finding is a further reason for the native-norm sensitivity analysis. It cautions against interpreting a pooled frequency coefficient as a property of the language when it is partly a property of the resource.

The register problem extends to surprisal, since language models whose training experience matches the reader’s predict reading times better than general-corpus models do (Škrjanec & Demberg, 2026). No child-register model exists for these languages under one procedure, so the mismatch is recorded as a limitation of the uniform primary resource, the Goldfish models. Their 1 GB tier is chosen on the psychometric grounds given in Supplementary Table 3. To measure the mismatch, we will report, for each language, the model’s mean per-word surprisal on the story texts and on a held-out 10 MB sample of its training corpus, OSCAR 21.09. The ratio of the two, reported with them, expresses the register gap on a scale comparable across languages and flags any language where the model is badly matched. None of these quantities involves an outcome.

Two properties of the surprisal pipeline are registered here because the fourth rule requires them to be frozen. Each text is scored in 512-token windows, the model’s maximum, at a stride of 256 tokens. A token’s surprisal is retained only from the window in which it has at least 256 tokens of preceding context, except at the start of a text, where every token is retained. Per language, we will report the mean and maximum subword-token count per text, the tokens-per-word ratio and the proportion of texts exceeding 512 tokens. The same rule requires per-language segmentation diagnostics: the mean and standard deviation of sentence length, the proportion of sentences ending without terminal punctuation, and boundary agreement between the two parsers’ splitters. Agreement is measured on the stratified sample of 50 texts per language drawn for the sixth outcome-neutral check. A native speaker’s adjudication of sentence boundaries, registered and reported with that check, completes these diagnostics.

The date of the Goldfish training snapshot favours this study, because OSCAR 21.09 derives from the February 2021 Common Crawl, which predates the generator that wrote these stories. A predictability model that could not have been fitted to text of this kind is a safeguard, whereas the same frozen-snapshot property counted against wordfreq as a measure of current usage. The classic stories, however, may be in the training corpus if they are public-domain texts. A model’s surprisal is lower on text it has seen than on text it has not. A classic text’s lexical component would then be pulled towards the easy end for a reason unrelated to the child. The per-language comparison of classic and generated surprisal, registered under Sensitivity analyses, checks for this bias. The refit on generated texts alone shows how far the bias could affect the estimates.

Every per-language claim in Supplementary Table 3 refers to the ten languages the structural sample showed: Dutch, English, French, German, Greek, Italian, Korean, Polish, Portuguese and Spanish. The app offers nine more, Arabic, Hebrew, Indonesian, Romanian, Russian, Simplified Chinese, Thai, Traditional Chinese and Turkish, and the extract may contain any of them. The same four rules apply to them, and Supplementary Table 4, after the paragraphs on Greek, gives their resources and the rules their scripts require. A language outside these 19 would be treated in the same way, with its resources reported before the feature inventory is frozen.

Korean has the fewest resources of the ten languages in the structural sample, though several of the app’s other languages have fewer (Supplementary Table 4). It lacks a subtitle frequency norm and any coverage in the cross-linguistic age-of-acquisition set. Its only published concreteness ratings cover 450 nouns from an affective word list, some of them names of neutral objects (Hong et al., 2016). Its four sets rated for valence and arousal hold 252 to 450 words each, mostly emotion terms (Lee et al., 2022), and imageability has been rated for 665 nouns (Park, 2004). At these sizes, none of the sets is expected to meet the 80 per cent token-coverage rule. Korean does have a uniform frequency estimate, a Goldfish model, both parsers, induced affect and a lexical decision megastudy of 30,930 words (Yi et al., 2017), which supplies decision times, not rated norms. Through the uniform resources, it contributes to the lexical and grammatical components and to the affective predictors, but to no analysis that requires a native norm. Korean also determined the parser tier, since Stanza publishes only nocharlm packages for all three of its Korean treebanks (see the dependency row of Supplementary Table 3).

The eojeol convention under Measures needs a separate rule for lexical lookups. The eojeol is the right unit for the outcome’s denominator and for the parser-derived grammatical features, since the Korean treebanks are tokenised on whitespace. For a frequency lookup, however, it is the wrong unit: a surface eojeol carries its particles, so most eojeol forms will be absent from any word list. For the lexical features that require a lookup, Korean text is segmented into morphemes with a named morphological analyser whose version is frozen with the inventory. The lookup is performed on content morphemes, which the source list can match, and the values are aggregated back to the eojeol and then to the text mean. Both matched-token proportions, at eojeol and at morpheme level, are reported before the features are frozen. Without this rule, Korean would be expected to fail the token-coverage rule for frequency and to contribute no lexical feature.

In Korean, mean sentence length, mean dependency distance and mean orthographic length all rest on the eojeol, a unit that bundles a content word with its grammatical particles (Baek et al., 2023). For that reason, the Korean lexical slope is interpreted on its own, as Measures requires of every Korean association, and is not compared like for like with other languages’ lexical slopes. Hangul is written in syllable blocks. Korean kindergarten children identified consonant-vowel blocks better than consonant and vowel letters, and their identification of these blocks predicted word reading over and above letter knowledge (Cho, 2009). Among 74 children from kindergarten to grade 3 reading isolated words aloud, naming latency rose with length in both jamo (letters) and blocks, less steeply in older children (Ju et al., 2022). Once word frequency was modelled, neither count had a main effect. The jamo effect still declined with age, whereas the block effect varied with frequency but no longer reliably with age. Because the two counts were collinear, their effects were estimated in separate models. Among 94 university students, lexical decision latency did not differ significantly between two- and three-block words (Pae et al., 2020). Every block in these words had a final consonant, so they also differed in jamo, six against nine. The null result therefore applies equally to both counts.

Neither length study separates the two counts, so the choice between them rests on comparability. The jamo count will be the Korean length measure because it is a letter count like those of the other languages, and the block count will enter only the correlation described in Supplementary Table 3. Jamo are counted after Unicode canonical decomposition, which treats a doubled or compound letter as one jamo and a silent initial ㅇ as one. This convention reproduces the ranges of letter length reported by Ju et al. (2022). Both length findings come from isolated words, and whether length drives the lexical component in Korean text remains open. Korean’s congruence with the pooled component solution will therefore be reported whether or not Korean is among the five largest languages. Where Korean has fewer than the 100 distinct eligible texts required for a language-specific solution, the correlations of its features with the pooled component scores will be reported in place of its congruence.

Before the features are frozen, a Korean-reading collaborator will look for further Korean norms, because a search of Crossref is a poor instrument for Korean-language psycholinguistics. The collaborator will search the Korea Citation Index and RISS directly, with the Korean terms for concreteness (구체성), imageability (심상성, 심상가), age of acquisition (습득연령), valence (정서가) and arousal (각성가). The collaborator will also ask Tae and colleagues about the unpublished norming study behind the emotion ratings of their 1,544 words (Tae et al., 2026). Any larger norm set found that passes the token-coverage rule will enter the analyses that use a native norm for the same feature, as a recorded deviation from this protocol.

The classification of orthographic depth used in the main text (Seymour et al., 2003) omits Korean and Polish, so both are placed from other sources. The two placements are fixed with the feature inventory and will not be revised in the light of the per-language estimates. Two meta-analyses from one research group, both drawing on that classification, coded Korean as shallow for reading, and French and English as deep (Leachman et al., 2025; J. won Lee et al., 2023). The first coded Korean as deep for spelling, because Korean spelling preserves morphemes across resyllabification, which makes words harder to spell than to decode. The outcome here is reading time, so Korean will be annotated as shallow in the reading direction. Each meta-analysis coded depth as a dichotomy, so Korean will be placed shallower than French and English but not ranked among the seven sampled orthographies placed shallower than French, Polish included. Two further sources agree that Korean is shallower than English. The editors of a survey of seventeen orthographies give Finnish and Korean as examples of the most transparent alphabetic orthographies (Perfetti & Verhoeven, 2017). The Multilingual Eye-movement Corpus classes Korean as moderately transparent, beside Dutch and German (Siegelman et al., 2022). A benchmark of grapheme-to-phoneme conversion instead calls Hangul relatively deep, because its spelling leaves sound changes across syllable boundaries unmarked (Gorman et al., 2020).

In the judgement of Awramiuk & Krasowicz-Kupis (2013), Polish is less shallow than Italian or Finnish but less opaque than English or French. In grapheme-to-phoneme entropies computed over the same nouns in 16 languages, it lies between German and Portuguese, nearest Portuguese (Schüppert et al., 2017). The classification places German at the second level and Portuguese at the third. Polish will therefore be annotated at the middle level, beside Dutch and Portuguese. That placement is less secure than those taken from the classification itself. Polish is more consistent for reading than for spelling (Awramiuk & Krasowicz-Kupis, 2013; Łockiewicz & Ciecholewska, 2017), and one study of Polish children, citing the same entropies, groups it with Finnish, German and Greek as a transparent orthography (Dębska et al., 2019). One of the two meta-analyses codes Polish as deep, but only in an appendix table and without naming a source (Leachman et al., 2025).

The app’s other alphabetic languages are placed on the principle applied to Korean, by their consistency in the reading direction. Indonesian is grouped with Finnish and Italian as an orthography in which a letter is almost always pronounced the same way whatever the word (Borleffs et al., 2017, p. 1620). The Multilingual Eye-movement Corpus classes Turkish as transparent, beside Finnish, Greek, Italian and Spanish (Siegelman et al., 2022, Table 1). No comparative measure we found includes Romanian, but a study of its early readers describes its orthography as rather transparent, with most sounds written by a single letter (Dolean et al., 2021). Indonesian, Romanian and Turkish will therefore be annotated as shallow, the Romanian placement resting on the weakest evidence of the three. The same corpus classes Russian as moderately transparent, beside Dutch, German and Korean (Siegelman et al., 2022, Table 1). Russian spelling is, however, asymmetric: highly consistent from spelling to sound and much less so from sound to spelling, because of vowel reduction and voicing assimilation (Rakhlin et al., 2014). Like Korean, Russian will therefore be annotated as shallow in the reading direction. The five levels were built to order European alphabets, so Arabic, Hebrew, Thai and Chinese are not placed on them. Arabic and Hebrew are annotated as abjads, Thai as an abugida and Chinese, in both scripts, as morphosyllabic.

Greek is the next weakest in norms, though not in the suitability of its writing system. Its orthography is transparent enough for every word to be aligned completely between graphemes and phonemes, and that transparency has been quantified separately in the reading and the spelling direction (Protopapas & Vlahou, 2009). Greek children reach accurate foundation word reading within the first school year (Seymour et al., 2003). Among 234 Greek children followed through the first two grades, word reading fluency predicted accuracy by the second grade, but no path from accuracy to fluency was found (Georgiou et al., 2025). Greek has two sets rated for valence and arousal, and neither is expected to meet the token-coverage rule for the native-norm affective sensitivity analysis. One covers 348 words, rated for valence, arousal and other dimensions by 229 native Greek speakers at the University of Cyprus (Vaiouli et al., 2023). The other covers the 1,034 words of the Affective Norms for English Words, translated into Greek and rated anew by 105 native Greek speakers (Palogiannidi et al., 2016). For concreteness, Greek has a set of 2,948 words rated for concreteness and imageability, most of them nouns (Traikapi et al., 2024). The set is available only as a preprint, and its 312 concreteness raters were Greek-Cypriot adults. Both facts will be stated wherever a Greek concreteness value is reported.

The app’s nine other languages are covered by the same uniform resources, with three exceptions. subs2vec has no Chinese or Thai, so in those languages the primary frequency is computed from the OpenSubtitles 2018 corpus (Lison et al., 2018) after segmentation with the frozen word segmenter. That keeps the source register, subtitles, but not the pipeline, since the subs2vec documentation does not name the corpus release it used. Goldfish publishes no 1 GB model for Traditional Chinese, so Traditional Chinese text is converted to Simplified script with OpenCC, at a version frozen with the inventory, for every lexical lookup, for surprisal under the Simplified Chinese models and for the induced affective values, which are Simplified. The parse and the sentence encoders run on the text as served. EuroLLM-1.7B does not cover Hebrew, Indonesian or Thai, so the single-multilingual-model contrast runs without them. The other uniform resources cover all nine. Stanza publishes nocharlm packages for each, and UDPipe 2 has models for each, Thai only in its latest release. The byte premiums that scale each Goldfish training corpus run from 0.94 for Simplified Chinese to 2.74 for Thai.

Four rules for these scripts are registered with the inventory. First, as Measures sets out, a Chinese or Thai word is a token of the frozen word segmenter of the Stanza package fixed for the language, which segments the text before it is parsed. The same tokens serve the outcome’s denominator, the frequency counts and every lookup, so a segmentation error moves all of them together. Second, Arabic and Hebrew write the article and some prepositions and conjunctions as prefixes to the word they precede. For lexical lookups in these languages, the parser’s multi-word-token expansion splits each orthographic word, and the lookup is made on the content word. As under the morpheme rule for Korean, the value is aggregated back to the orthographic word and then to the text mean, and both matched-token proportions are reported. Third, a script that leaves short vowels unwritten does not show its syllables, so Arabic and Hebrew do not contribute to the predicted spoken duration, and the duration-adjusted contrast for Hypothesis 4 runs without them. Fourth, Chinese, Thai and Indonesian do not mark tense on the verb, so the past-tense quantity of narrativity is undefined there, and the composite is the mean of the two quantities that are defined.

Three further limits are recorded here. Published attachment scores for the Thai treebank are far below those of the other languages, and Stanza’s Thai package has no lemmatiser, so a Thai lemma is its surface form. Thai is therefore expected to fail the labelled-attachment criterion of the sixth outcome-neutral check and to leave the grammatical component, and the check decides. Turkish expresses some connective relations with converbal suffixes, which a match on whole tokens cannot count, so Turkish connective densities cover free forms only. Finally, the stories in every language but English are machine translations of the English texts (Table 2 of the main text). A text property measured in a translation is a property of that translation, so the cross-language comparisons registered here compare translations of one set of stories, not texts written independently in each language.

Every affective value in this design descends from adult ratings. The induced resource is trained on adult English norms and propagated to the other languages, and every native set named in Supplementary Table 3 is adult-rated. The readers are children of 7 and over, and where child norms exist, the difference between child and adult ratings is age-graded across this study’s two age bands. The overlap of valence and arousal with LexicalDifficulty, given under Measures, is one reason for entering them as main effects only, and adult rating is another. A covariate measured on an adult referent cannot separate a developmental change in reading from a developmental change in how a word is rated. For the languages in which a child-rated set is available, we will recompute mean valence and mean arousal from it at rating ages inside each band. Their correlations with the primary means and with each other will be reported, whatever they show, as a descriptive check that enters no model.

Several tools named in this section have no registered DOI, as is usual for research software distributed through model repositories. Such tools are identified by the checkpoint that the fourth rule under Materials freezes, and the identifiers are deposited with the feature-extraction code.

S5. The design analysis in full

The precision analysis simulated data from the design specifications deposited in design_analysis/, and design_analysis/run_design_analysis.R reproduces it (Bernabeu, 2026a). The main text describes the three simulated models and what their omissions imply. This section records how the models were fitted and how the requirements were derived. The tier-1 and narration models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood with lme4, with its derivative-based convergence checks switched off. Each simulated tier-2 child read five distinct texts in a random order, one at each session position. The accumulated-reading contrast has a standard deviation of one across the five positions, so its coefficient is on the per-standard-deviation unit that Hypothesis 6 registers. In the texts sweeps, each simulated reader drew one text at random, so twelve readings per text is an average and individual texts received more or fewer.

Each requirement comes from a probit regression of the decision probability on the square root of the swept quantity, with each grid point weighted by its replicate count. The fitted curve is inverted at the target, and the delta method gives the requirement’s 95 per cent interval. Where the two-parameter curve fits the grid worse than its replicate counts imply, the interval is widened by Pearson’s heterogeneity factor. A target that the simulated range does not reach is reported as not reached, and the fitted curve is never extrapolated. The texts and narration sweeps used 200 replicates per grid point, and the readings, null and tier-2 sweeps used 120. At two readings per text, 4 of the 120 fits for each coefficient were singular and were retained in the widths and probabilities. The pooled grid is in design_analysis/precision_sweep.csv. The per-sweep files under design_analysis/sweeps/ add, for each grid point, the Monte Carlo standard error on each decision probability, the number of fits attempted and how many converged, were singular or raised warnings. Supplementary Figure 2 shows the narration and within-child curves that the main text summarises.

For comparison with Nature Human Behaviour’s a priori power floor of 0.95 for frequentist plans, design_analysis/solve_at_095.R recomputes the requirements at that level from the same archived curves, without re-simulating, and writes them to minimum_texts_095.csv. At that target, the main effects need 926 distinct texts for lexical difficulty (95 per cent interval 794 to 1,069), 812 for grammatical load (723 to 906) and 784 for referential continuity (696 to 877). The grammatical-load interaction with the age band needs 1,510 texts (1,349 to 1,680), the narration interaction 1,506 (1,349 to 1,672) and the within-child term 731 children in tier 2 (621 to 851). The continuity interaction did not reach 0.95 within the 1,600 texts simulated.

For the Bayes-factor criterion registered under Decision rules, the requirements were computed from the same archived curves by design_analysis/solve_at_bf10.R, again without re-simulating. The reference prior is a Student t with 3 degrees of freedom, centred at zero, with scale 0.5 for a main effect and 0.25 for an interaction. Under it, the prior mass inside the region of practical equivalence is 7.3 per cent for a main effect and 14.6 per cent for an interaction. A Bayes factor of 10 for the region outside is therefore reached when the posterior mass inside falls to 0.8 and 1.7 per cent. For a normal posterior, those masses place the estimate 2.42 and 2.13 standard errors beyond the region’s edge. The normal approximation is applied at each grid point. The mean interval width gives the standard error, and the probability that a true effect of 0.10 clears that distance is the normal tail beyond it. The resulting curve is inverted at 0.90 and 0.95 as in solve_at_095.R, and the requirements are written to minimum_texts_bf10.csv.

The approximation is checked against the simulation at the simulation’s own criterion. At every grid point, the approximated probability of an interval wholly outside the region lies within 0.06 of the simulated one (sweeps/bf10_calibration.csv). Applied at that criterion, the same approximation puts the 0.90 requirement at about 715 texts for each main effect, against the simulated 738, 660 and 634, whose spread is Monte Carlo variation. Relative to the simulation’s criterion, the Bayes-factor criterion raises the requirement by a factor of about 1.3 for a main effect and for the tier-2 term, and by about 1.1 for an interaction. The Bayes-factor requirements are reported under Design and precision analysis and in Table 1 of the main text.

The variance shares in the design specifications are assumptions, as Design and precision analysis states, so design_analysis/run_variance_sensitivity.R re-derives the tier-1 requirements with the share of variance between texts at 5, 13 and 20 per cent. The script holds the child variance at the specifications’ value and the total variance at one, so the residual absorbs each change. It re-runs the texts sweeps for Hypotheses 1 to 4 with 200 replicates per grid point and solves each curve as the main analysis does, under the simulation’s criterion and the Bayes-factor criterion, at 0.90 and 0.95. The rows at 9 per cent are read from the main analysis’s cached sweeps and reproduce its figures. The 20 per cent sweeps were run on the University of Oxford’s Advanced Research Computing cluster with the package versions of the main analysis. A re-run there of both 5 per cent sweeps reproduced the local results in every replicate decision, with mean interval widths agreeing to within \(10^{-9}\). The results are in design_analysis/variance_sensitivity.csv, and the software versions in session_info_variance_sensitivity.txt and session_info_variance_sensitivity_arc.txt.

At 0.90 under the simulation’s criterion, the three main effects needed 489 to 553 distinct texts at a text share of 5 per cent, 634 to 738 at the registered 9 per cent, 753 to 946 at 13 per cent and 1,146 to 1,243 at 20 per cent. Under the Bayes-factor criterion, they needed about 720, 930, 1,140 and 1,490, and at 20 per cent none reached 0.95 within the 1,600 texts simulated. A main effect is identified between texts, so its requirement rises with the variance between them. The two age-band interactions and the narration interaction moved the other way, and by less. At 0.90, they needed 1,256, 1,498 and 1,276 texts at 5 per cent and 1,141, 1,224 and 1,145 at 20 per cent. An interaction with a reader attribute compares readings of the same texts, from which the text intercept cancels, and with the total held at one, a larger text share leaves less variance within texts. The text share can be estimated only from a fitted model, so it enters none of the registered adjustments. Its estimate, the Text-level intraclass correlation reported under Measures, will show where on this range the extract lies.

The ordinal outcome of Hypothesis 5 was simulated and fitted with a separate script, design_analysis/run_ordinal_analysis.R, because the routine used for the reading-time models cannot fit a cumulative logit. Treating the three-category rating as continuous would avoid that problem but answer a different question. The script generates the rating from the same design specification, with each replicate seeded deterministically from its grid point and replicate index. On the latent logistic scale, whose residual variance is fixed at \(\pi^2/3\), the specification’s standard deviations give the text intercept about 2.5 per cent of the latent variance and the child intercept about 5.7 per cent, so the shares of 9 and 20 per cent stated in the main text describe the reading-time models only. The two thresholds, at -0.85 and 0.85 on that scale, are a placeholder that puts roughly 30, 40 and 30 per cent of ratings in the three categories, and the fourth adjustment in the main text replaces them with the observed proportions. It keeps each reading’s rating with probability 0.30, the availability that the structural sample showed, and applies the protocol’s five-rater floor inside every replicate. The model fitted with ordinal::clmm is a cumulative-logit counterpart of the registered model, containing only the three components, the age band and a text intercept. On the log-odds scale, the region is symmetric about zero, with the half-width of log(1.05) that the main text registers. Only a determinate verdict in the direction of the true effect counts as a success. The ordinal sweep used 60 replicates per grid point. In 14, 8, 4 and 1 of them at 200, 400, 800 and 1,600 texts, the text variance was estimated at zero and the standard errors were undefined. Those replicates are not a random subset, so none was discarded. Each was refitted as the fixed-effects cumulative logit to which the mixed model reduces at that boundary.

The rating sets the most demanding requirement in the design. At 200, 400, 800 and 1,600 generated texts, with an average of twelve readings each, the 95 per cent interval on a coefficient whose true value is 0.10 log odds has a mean width of 0.42 to 0.44, 0.30, 0.21 and 0.15. The probability of a determinate verdict does not exceed 0.30 at any grid point, so the 0.90 target is not reached within the range simulated, and no requirement is obtained by inverting the curve. The grid does, however, establish how interval width scales with the number of texts, with fitted exponents between -0.505 and -0.524 against a theoretical -0.5. A requirement projected from that scaling must also allow for attenuation, because the simulation generates child variance that the fitted model omits. In a logit model, that omission shrinks every coefficient towards zero, by a factor of about 0.967 at the specification’s child standard deviation of 0.45 (Zeger et al., 1988). The mean fitted estimates across the grid, 0.093 to 0.095 in absolute value with Monte Carlo standard errors of 0.004, agree with that factor. A determinate verdict must then clear the region’s boundary starting from the attenuated value of 0.097, and doing so requires a mean interval width of 0.058. On the fitted scaling law, that width is reached at roughly 9,300 to 10,300 generated texts, of which about 2,700 to 3,000 have at least five ratings. Without the attenuation, the projection would be 8,200 to 9,000. Against the 634 to 738 texts that the three components need on reading time at the same criterion and target, the rating demands an order of magnitude more. The difference follows from a three-category response observed for 30 per cent of readings. These projections depend on the rating’s availability across the full window and on how heavily texts are reused, which the provider-computed counts will supply. Before any outcome model is fitted, the simulation will be re-run at the observed values of both and at the category proportions observed in the extract. Those proportions matter because they fix the spacing of the simulated thresholds and with it the information a cumulative logit can recover. Design and precision analysis registers the re-runs at the observed availability and proportions as its third and fourth adjustments. Every number in this paragraph is reproduced by design_analysis/summarise_ordinal.R, which pools the stored grid-point results.

S6. Deriving the priors from the systematic review

The main text states under Priors which coefficients receive review-derived priors, when the rules of the prior-derivation pipeline were frozen and when the pipeline will be run. The rules are deposited verbatim as priors/prior_derivation.md, and the README of the preregistration directory records the revision copied and the copy’s SHA-256 checksum. They were preceded in June 2026 by test extractions from 12 papers, made under an earlier schema and later used to refine the extraction instructions. An automated listing of the statistics reported in the included papers began in the same month. Here we summarise the fixed assignment of the review’s extracted effects to this protocol’s coefficients, the metric families and the pooling and de-duplication rules. LexicalDifficulty pools the review’s word-frequency, lexical-coverage, other-lexical and lexical-diversity effects on reading. Before pooling, these are sign-harmonised so that frequency and coverage are reversed to the direction of difficulty, whereas age of acquisition, word length and surprisal keep their sign. Age-of-acquisition effects remain in the pool, and so partly shape the prior’s centre, because the confirmatory component omits that feature only for want of norms comparable across languages. GrammaticalLoad pools syntactic-complexity and sentence-length effects. ReferentialContinuity pools coherence effects. The rules assign no review evidence to any secondary predictor, so the effects of discourse structure and of emotionality that the review extracts inform no prior here.

Pooling keeps metric families apart, because a conversion that is mathematically available is not always a valid one. The primary prior for a coefficient uses only standardised regression coefficients reported on the mapped predictor and outcome. No other metric is admitted to reach the two papers that the main text requires for a derived prior. Correlations, partial correlations recovered from t or from one-degree-of-freedom F statistics, standardised mean differences and odds ratios form separately named sensitivity pools, and no generic conversion from an odds ratio or a d to a correlation is applied to a continuous predictor. Estimates are pooled within an outcome class and never across classes, so a reading-time coefficient draws only on reading-time evidence and the rating coefficient only on rated difficulty. Unstandardised coefficients without convertible scaling, and rows whose direction the extraction could not resolve, are listed with the reason for their exclusion.

Within each combination of coefficient, outcome class and metric family, a random-effects meta-analysis fitted by restricted maximum likelihood pools one estimate per paper per relation, the one the extraction marks as primary, with the paper as the cluster. Labelled subgroups and alternative specifications are never treated as independent studies. A sensitivity analysis keeps every eligible estimate and models their dependence in a multilevel model (Cheung, 2019), and a Bayesian random-effects model is a second. The primary pool is restricted to samples aged 7 to 10. A moderated pool adds samples aged 11 to 18 or spanning both ranges, with the age stratum as a moderator, and its estimate is reported with Hypotheses 2 and 3 as the review’s own evidence on whether text-feature effects change with age. Where the primary pool has fewer than two papers for a coefficient but the moderated pool has at least two, the moderated estimate is used and the substitution is stated. For Hypothesis 6, studies of oral reading rate enter only a sensitivity pool, because the app does not record whether a child read silently or aloud. Where a meta-analysis in the corpus subsumes three or more of the other papers, the pooled row will be preferred and those papers dropped. Otherwise, those papers will be used and the pooled row dropped. In either case, the alternative will be reported as a sensitivity analysis.

The prior is then Student’s \(t(3, \hat\mu, \sigma)\), where \(\hat\mu\) is the pooled estimate on the model’s scale and \(\sigma = 2\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu)^2 + \hat\tau^2}\), with a floor of 0.10. The heavy tails let the data override a misplaced centre. For each component, we will record from the pipeline’s output whether \(|\hat\mu|\) falls below 0.10 standard deviations, the comparison that decides whether the sixth adjustment to the main text’s precision thresholds runs. The frozen rules do not contain this step, which this protocol adds. The timestamped output will be deposited before the extract is transferred. The complete prior table, forest plots, conversion arithmetic and exclusion appendix will accompany the Stage 2 report.
