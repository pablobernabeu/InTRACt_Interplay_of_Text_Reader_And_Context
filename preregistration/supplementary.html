Supplementary material: Text properties and children’s reading pace in a multilingual storytelling app

S1. The theory object

The constructs, causal claims, auxiliary assumptions, rival accounts and predictions behind this protocol are held in a single machine-readable file, theory/intract.theory.yaml, which the theoryforge package reads and checks (Bernabeu, 2026b). Figure 2 of the main text is drawn from that file. Running theory/build_theory.R reproduces the checklist scores, the redundancy screen and the implications in this section, theory/check_manuscript_claims.R prints the counts quoted here, and theory/make_figure1.R draws Figure 2.

The object registers twelve constructs, each with a definition, its intended measurement and its boundary conditions. Eighteen propositions link them, each stating its mechanism as well as its direction. Fifteen of the eighteen are directed edges of the causal graph, and the other three are moderation claims. Six auxiliary assumptions are recorded as entries in their own right, five of them corresponding to questions put to the data controller and listed in Table 2 of the main text. Six rival accounts are registered, and seven of the ten predictions are marked diagnostic against one of them.

The rigour checklist scores the object at 90.7 out of 100 with no blocking failure, and every item passes. On falsifiability and precision, at least one prediction forbids an observation, all ten predictions are interval predictions, and their mean severity clears the threshold. On construct quality, every construct carries a definition, a measurement and boundary conditions, no pair exceeds the lexical redundancy screen (largest observed similarity 0.17), and the theory’s scope is stated. On structure, every proposition states a mechanism, at least one relation is causal, auxiliary assumptions are fewer than propositions with none added defensively in response to a prediction, and every prediction is reachable from a proposition in the graph. Finally, at least one prediction discriminates between registered alternatives, and a formal-model reference points at the design specification used for the precision analysis.

Because the causal graph is acyclic it yields testable implications, thirty of them, of which three are tested in the fourth outcome-neutral quality check of the main text. The most consequential is that the three text properties should be mutually independent once the reading level and the generation route are conditioned on. A first version of the object, lacking the text-provenance construct, implied that they were independent unconditionally, which is false of any real corpus: a text’s target reading level shifts all three property families at once. Adding text provenance as a common cause of all three, and giving it an incoming path from the reader’s own decoding automaticity, both repaired that implication and derived the adjustment set the analysis uses.

Of the three implications carried into the fourth outcome-neutral quality check, the third is the one for which the literature gives the most specific reason to expect failure. A reported feeling of difficulty is a metacognitive experience drawing on affective as well as cognitive input (Efklides, 2006), and in 274 pupils of grades 7 to 9 solving mathematical problems it varied across the phases of a task and bore on performance only indirectly, through ideas about what the task required (Efklides et al., 1999). In 80 children aged 8 to 12, 38 of them with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder, ratings of task difficulty and of effort given after a battery of cognitive tasks were uncorrelated with each other and only weakly related to performance (Torres & Toplak, 2026), and among college readers such judgements often rest on surface features of a text, cues that track how well it was understood poorly (Thiede et al., 2010). What a failure of this implication identifies, and how the Hypothesis 5 odds ratios are then reported, is registered with the fourth quality check in the main text.

S3. Language resources for the text features

A multilingual design faces a measurement problem that a monolingual one does not. Concreteness taken from a 40,000-item English rating study, a 350,000-lemma German regression, a 1,400-item Spanish rating study and a 77-language embedding prediction are four different measurements wearing one label. If a resource is chosen language by language on the grounds of local quality, then the method of measurement is perfectly confounded with the language, and standardising within language removes the difference in location and scale but not the difference in construct. There are two clean responses: use one uniformly produced resource everywhere, accepting lower absolute quality for higher comparability, or use native norms and treat every cross-language comparison as descriptive. We take the first, because language enters the confirmatory models as a grouping factor with varying slopes, and those slopes have to mean the same thing in every language. The native-norm sensitivity analysis and the per-language correlation between the two measures are specified in the main text. That correlation is the quantity that tells a reader how much the choice mattered.

Two widely used resources are excluded by that rule, and the reasons are recorded because each is the obvious candidate for its family. The wordfreq package is not used as the primary frequency source: its data are a frozen snapshot of usage to about 2021, its maintainer discontinued it in September 2024 to avoid ingesting post-2022 web text contaminated by generated language, and its wordlists for Korean and Greek are built from fewer sources than the rest. The non-English editions of the NRC Valence, Arousal and Dominance lexicon are not used at all: they are machine translations of the English norms made in 2022, not ratings collected in the target languages, and their licence is non-commercial.

A subtitle corpus indexes adult exposure, and the register mismatch for developing readers is a limitation that no available uniform resource removes. A recent demonstration that frequency estimates from a large-language-model corpus prompted for child-directed text fit young German readers better than child-book norms did (Schepens et al., 2025) sharpens that point without changing the choice: a generated corpus is neither frozen nor documented in the way a subtitle pipeline is, and the same study notes that such corpora show atypical linguistic patterns. We therefore keep subs2vec as the primary resource. Wherever a native child-register norm exists for a language, we will report the per-language correlation between the primary frequency estimate and that norm, so that the cost of the adult register is visible in the language where it can be measured. The choice of frequency database is not neutral for reading-time fit: pooling five self-paced reading studies in German, subtitle frequencies did not fit sentential reading times best, a written-corpus database did, and the ranking of databases differed by participant population (Haeuser & Kray, 2025). That is an argument for reporting the per-language correlation and the native-norm sensitivity analysis, and against reading a pooled frequency coefficient as a property of the language when it is partly a property of the resource. The same point applies to surprisal, where language models whose training experience matches the reader’s predict reading times better than general-corpus models do (Škrjanec & Demberg, 2026). No child-register model exists for these ten languages under one procedure, so the register mismatch is recorded here as a limitation of the uniform resource, and the Goldfish tier is chosen on the psychometric grounds given in Supplementary Table 3. The mismatch is measurable, and we will measure it. For each of the ten, we will report the model’s mean per-word surprisal on the study’s story texts beside its mean per-word surprisal on a held-out 10 MB sample of the same OSCAR 21.09 data, together with the ratio of the two. The ratio makes the register gap visible and comparable across languages and flags a language in which the model is badly matched, and neither quantity involves an outcome.

Two properties of the surprisal pipeline are registered here because the fourth rule under Materials requires them to be frozen. Each text is scored in 512-token windows, the model’s maximum, at a stride of 256 tokens, and a token’s surprisal is retained only from the window in which it carries at least 256 tokens of preceding context, except at the start of a text where every token is retained. Per language, we will report the mean and maximum subword tokens per text, the tokens-per-word ratio and the proportion of texts exceeding 512 tokens. The sentence segmentation diagnostics that the same rule requires are these: per language, the mean and standard deviation of sentence length, the proportion of sentences ending without terminal punctuation, and boundary agreement between the two parsers’ splitters on a stratified sample, with a native-speaker adjudication of twenty texts per language reported alongside the hand-checked parse. The training snapshot also cuts the right way for this study. OSCAR 21.09 derives from the February 2021 Common Crawl, which predates the generator that wrote these stories, so a predictability model that could not have been fitted to text of this kind is a safeguard here, where the same frozen-snapshot property counted against wordfreq as a measure of current usage. The classic stories cut the other way. If they are public-domain texts, the training corpus may contain them, and a model’s surprisal on text it has seen is lower than on text it has not, so a classic text’s lexical component would be pulled towards the easy end for a reason that has nothing to do with the child. The per-language comparison of classic and generated surprisal registered under Sensitivity analyses in the main text is the check, and the refit on generated texts alone bounds the consequence.

Supplementary Table 3 gives the primary resource for each feature family, the sensitivity resource, and the coverage across the ten languages the structural sample showed. Those ten languages are Dutch, English, French, German, Greek, Italian, Korean, Polish, Portuguese and Spanish, and every per-language claim in the table refers to that list. Where the export turns out to carry more languages, the same rules are applied to them and the table is extended to cover them.

Korean has the fewest resources of the ten languages, and we record the consequences here in advance. Of the seven norm-based feature families, Korean lacks a subtitle frequency norm and any coverage in the cross-linguistic age-of-acquisition set, and its rated concreteness and valence and arousal norms are sets of 267 to 450 words published in Korean-language journals, too small to meet the 80 per cent coverage rule. What it does have is a uniform frequency estimate, a Goldfish model, both parsers, induced affect and a lexical decision megastudy of 30,930 words (Yi et al., 2017). Korean therefore contributes to the lexical and grammatical components and to the affective predictors through the uniform resources, and contributes to no analysis that requires a native norm.

Korean is also the reason the parser tier is fixed as it is. Stanza publishes only nocharlm packages for Korean, across all three of its Korean treebanks, where it offers Greek a Greek BERT and Dutch a Dutch RoBERTa, so a policy of taking each language’s best package would have left Korean at the weakest tier while every other language improved, and would have confounded the parser with the language exactly as the uniformity rule forbids.

One consequence of the eojeol convention has to be registered separately, because it cuts the other way from the rest. The eojeol is the right unit for the outcome’s denominator and for the parser-derived grammatical features, since the Korean treebanks are tokenised on whitespace, but it is the wrong unit for a frequency lookup: a surface eojeol carries its particles, so most eojeol forms will be absent from any word list. For the lexical features that require a lookup, Korean text is therefore segmented into morphemes with a named morphological analyser whose version is frozen with the inventory, the lookup is performed on content morphemes, and the values are aggregated back to the eojeol and then to the text mean, so the text-level quantity stays per-eojeol while the lookup runs on a unit the source list can match. Both matched-token proportions, at eojeol and at morpheme level, are reported before the features are frozen. Without that rule, Korean would be expected to fail the token-coverage rule for frequency and to contribute no lexical feature at all.

Korean is also the one language whose orthographic length is not a letter count, so its length feature measures something different from the other nine, and its lexical slope is read only through the language-varying slopes, never as a like-for-like comparison of a length coefficient. Korean’s per-word quantities are per-eojeol quantities throughout, so its mean sentence length and mean dependency distance, like its orthographic length, count a unit that bundles a content word with its grammatical particles (Baek et al., 2023). Hangul is written in syllable blocks, and the block is the unit Korean children acquire first, predicting Hangul word reading over and above letter knowledge (Cho, 2009), which is why the primary length count is the block and the jamo count is the sensitivity measure. Whether length carries the lexical component in Korean is left open, since in adult Hangul lexical decision frequency predicted latency where length in syllables did not, on a two-to-three-syllable contrast in skilled readers (Pae et al., 2020). Korean’s congruence with the pooled component solution will therefore be reported whether or not Korean is among the five largest languages, and where Korean falls below the hundred-text rule for a language-specific solution, the correlations of its features with the pooled component scores are reported in its place.

Two tasks fall to a Korean-reading collaborator before the features are frozen, because a search of Crossref is a poor instrument for Korean-language psycholinguistics. The first is to search the Korea Citation Index and RISS directly; any larger norm set found there will be added to the sensitivity analysis, with the addition recorded as a deviation from this protocol. The second is to supply a published source for the orthographic depth of Hangul, which the European classifications the main text uses do not cover, so that Korean can be placed when the per-language interactions are ordered by depth.

Greek is the next weakest in norms, though not in the suitability of its writing system. Greek is transparent enough for every word to be aligned completely between graphemes and phonemes, and transparency has been quantified for it separately in the reading and the spelling direction (Protopapas & Vlahou, 2009). Greek children reach accurate foundation word reading within the first school year (Seymour et al., 2003), and among 234 Greek children followed from the start of the first grade to the end of the second, word reading fluency predicted accuracy by the second grade, while the path from accuracy to fluency was not found (Georgiou et al., 2025). Its rated affect sets are small, 348 words in one and 1,034 translated items in another, so the native-norm affective sensitivity analysis for Greek is subject to the coverage rule above. For concreteness, a set of about 2,950 words rated for concreteness and imageability is available, which puts Greek in the same band as French, Italian and Spanish (Traikapi et al., 2024). It is a preprint and its raters were Greek-Cypriot, and both facts are stated wherever a Greek concreteness value is reported.

French and Portuguese are otherwise well resourced but absent from both age-of-acquisition papers, so age of acquisition contributes for them only through the broad native sets, where those exist.

One limitation runs across every affective value in this design. All of them descend from adult ratings: the induced resource is trained on adult English norms and propagated to the other nine languages, and every native set named in Supplementary Table 3 is adult-rated. The readers are children of 7 and over, and where child norms exist the difference is age-graded across this study’s own split. For the languages in which a child-rated set is available, we will recompute mean valence and mean arousal from it at rating ages inside each band, and report the correlations with the primary means and with each other. That check is descriptive, enters no model, and is reported whatever it shows. It is also why valence and arousal enter as main effects only, since a covariate measured on an adult referent cannot separate a developmental change in reading from a developmental change in how a word is rated.

Every tool will be recorded by name, version and, where the tool is a model, by the exact checkpoint. Several of the tools named here have no registered DOI, which is normal for research software distributed through model repositories. Those are identified by checkpoint instead, and the identifiers are deposited with the feature-extraction code.

S5. The design analysis in full

The precision analysis simulated data from the portable design specifications deposited in design_analysis/, and design_analysis/run_design_analysis.R reproduces it (Bernabeu, 2026a). The main text describes the three simulated models, what they omit and what follows from the omissions, and this section records the fitting and solving details. The tier-1 and narration models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood with lme4, with its derivative-based convergence checks switched off. Each simulated tier-2 child read five distinct texts in a random order, one at each session position, and the accumulated-reading contrast has a standard deviation of one across the five positions, so its coefficient is on the per-standard-deviation unit that Hypothesis 6 registers. In the texts sweeps each simulated reader draws one text at random, so twelve readings per text is an average and individual texts receive more or fewer.

Each requirement and its 95 per cent interval come from a probit regression of the decision probability on the square root of the swept quantity, weighted by the replicates behind each grid point and inverted at the target by the delta method. Where the two-parameter curve fits the grid worse than its replicate counts imply, the interval is widened by Pearson’s heterogeneity factor. A target that the simulated range does not reach is reported as not reached and is never extrapolated. The pooled grid is in design_analysis/precision_sweep.csv. The per-sweep files under design_analysis/sweeps/ add, for each grid point, the Monte Carlo standard error on each decision probability, the number of fits attempted and the counts of converged, singular and warning fits. At two readings per text, 4 of the 120 fits for each coefficient were singular, and these were retained in the widths and probabilities. The texts and narration sweeps used 200 replicates per grid point, and the readings, null and tier-2 sweeps used 120. Supplementary Figure 2 shows the narration and within-child curves that the main text summarises.

Nature Human Behaviour names 0.95 as the a priori power floor for frequentist plans. We register 0.90 for this estimation design, for the reasons given in the main text, and design_analysis/solve_at_095.R re-solves the same archived curves at 0.95 without re-simulating anything, writing minimum_texts_095.csv. At that target the requirements are 926 distinct texts for lexical difficulty (95 per cent interval 794 to 1,069), 812 for grammatical load (723 to 906), 784 for referential continuity (696 to 877), 1,510 for the grammatical-load interaction with the age band (1,349 to 1,680), 1,506 for the narration interaction (1,349 to 1,672) and 731 children in tier 2 for the within-child term (621 to 851). The continuity interaction did not reach 0.95 within the 1,600 texts simulated.

The ordinal outcome behind Hypothesis 5 is handled by a separate script, design_analysis/run_ordinal_analysis.R, because a cumulative logit cannot be fitted by the routine the timing models use and treating a three-category rating as continuous would answer a different question. The script generates the rating from the same design specification, with each replicate seeded deterministically from its grid point and replicate index. It retains each reading with the 30 per cent availability the structural sample showed, applies the protocol’s five-rater floor inside every replicate, and fits a cumulative-logit counterpart of the registered model with ordinal::clmm, carrying the three components, the age band and a text intercept. The region of practical equivalence is applied symmetrically on the log-odds scale as a half-width of log(1.05), the region the main text registers, and only a determinate verdict in the direction of the true effect counts. The ordinal sweep used 60 replicates per grid point. In 14, 8, 4 and 1 of them at 200, 400, 800 and 1,600 texts, the text variance was estimated at zero and the standard errors were undefined. Those replicates are not a random subset, so each was refitted as the fixed-effects cumulative logit to which the mixed model reduces at that boundary and retained, and no replicate was discarded.

The result is the most demanding requirement in the design. At 200, 400, 800 and 1,600 generated texts, with an average of twelve readings each, the 95 per cent interval on a coefficient whose true value is 0.10 log odds has a mean width of 0.42 to 0.44, 0.30, 0.21 and 0.15, and the probability of a determinate verdict never rises above 0.30. The target of 0.90 is therefore not reached within the range simulated, and no crossing is quoted from it. What the grid does establish is the scaling. The fitted exponents of interval width on the number of texts lie between -0.505 and -0.524, against a theoretical -0.5. The projection also has to allow for attenuation. The simulation generates child variance that the fitted model omits, and in a logit model that omission shrinks every coefficient towards zero, by a factor of about 0.967 at the specification’s child standard deviation of 0.45 (Zeger et al., 1988). The mean fitted estimates across the grid, 0.093 to 0.095 in absolute value with Monte Carlo standard errors of 0.004, agree with that factor. A determinate verdict therefore has to clear the region boundary starting from 0.097, so the mean interval width it requires is 0.058. On the fitted scaling law that width is reached at roughly 9,300 to 10,300 generated texts, of which about 2,700 to 3,000 carry at least five ratings. Without the attenuation the projection would be 8,200 to 9,000. Set against the 738 texts the same coefficient needs on reading time, the rating demands an order of magnitude more. That is the cost of a three-category response observed for 30 per cent of readings. Both quantities depend on the rating’s availability across the full window and on how heavily texts are reused, which the provider-computed counts will supply. The simulation will be re-run at the observed values before any outcome model is fitted, and the proportions of the three rating categories will be reset from the extract at that re-run as well, since they fix the spacing of the simulated thresholds and with it the information a cumulative logit can extract. design_analysis/summarise_ordinal.R pools the cached grid points and reproduces every number in this paragraph.

S6. Deriving the priors from the systematic review

Priors for the confirmatory main effects and for the secondary text predictors come from the systematic review accompanying this project, through the pipeline whose freeze-and-run discipline is stated under Priors in the main text. This section records what the main text does not: the mapping from the review’s extracted effects to this protocol’s coefficients, the conversion order, and the pooling and de-duplication rules. The mapping is one-to-many and is fixed as follows. LexicalDifficulty pools the review’s word-frequency, lexical-coverage, other-lexical and lexical-diversity effects on reading, sign-harmonised so that frequency and coverage are flipped to a difficulty direction while age of acquisition, word length and surprisal keep their sign. GrammaticalLoad pools syntactic-complexity and sentence-length effects. ReferentialContinuity pools coherence effects, with discourse-structure rows informing the secondary narrativity predictor instead. Valence and arousal pool the text-emotionality rows separately where the extraction distinguishes them.

Estimates will be put on a common scale in a fixed order of preference. A standardised beta or a correlation goes through Fisher’s z. A d or g converts to r using the effective sample size, and an odds ratio converts to d and then to r. Where no effect size is reported but a test statistic is, r is recovered from t and its degrees of freedom and signed by the reported direction. Unstandardised coefficients without convertible scale information will not be pooled and will be listed in an exclusion appendix, as will rows whose direction the extraction could not resolve. Pooling will be a random-effects meta-analysis within each combination of coefficient and outcome class, with one estimate per paper per relation, so that labelled subgroups of a single paper are never treated as independent. Where a meta-analysis in the corpus subsumes three or more of the corpus’s own primary studies, the pooled row will be preferred and the overlapping primaries dropped. Otherwise, the primaries will be used and the pooled row dropped, and the alternative will be reported as a sensitivity analysis.

The prior is then Student’s \(t(3, \hat\mu, \sigma)\) with \(\hat\mu\) the pooled estimate on the model’s scale and \(\sigma = 2\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\mu)^2 + \hat\tau^2}\), floored at 0.10. The heavy tails let the data override a misplaced centre. The complete prior table, the forest plots, the conversion arithmetic and the list of excluded rows will accompany the Stage 2 report, and the pipeline’s output is timestamped and deposited before the extract is transferred. The same output records, for each component, whether the pooled estimate on the model’s scale falls below 0.10 standard deviations, which is the comparison the main text’s precision thresholds depend on.

S8. The structural sample

This section reports in full the pilot material described under Pilot data in the main text: the 35-row structural sample, the data dictionary and the summary computed from the sample with the count script. The facts the sample established are these. Every one of the 35 sessions was a finished reading, with the last page reached equal to the page count, so the extract is a record of completed readings. Narration was on in 32 of the 35 sessions. The post-reading difficulty rating was present in 10 sessions and absent in 25. The age band was blank in 10 sessions, and the recorded bands skewed towards children under 7, which is why the protocol’s inclusion criterion of 7 and older is expected to remove a substantial share of the export. The sessions covered ten reading languages. The per-session activity counters summed to more than the whole reading duration in 6 of the 14 sessions with any activity, in one case to nearly twice it, which is why those fields are set aside. Reading times were recorded as whole seconds.

Supplementary Table 4 tabulates the 35 rows by field, and Supplementary Table 5 reproduces the reading-time check from the summary that the count script wrote on 6 August 2026. The script is deposited with the protocol in count_script/, in the version supplied on 7 August 2026, which wrote that summary, and in a revised version dated 17 September 2026 that adds checks against malformed input without changing what is counted. Re-running either version on the sample reproduces that summary line for line apart from the date of the run, the revised version adding seventeen rows of its own. That summary regenerates Supplementary Table 5 and the rows of Supplementary Table 4 for the columns the script reads. The three activity-counter rows and the comparison of those counters with the reading duration were tallied directly from the sample, since the script does not read those fields. One further fact about the file bears on the transfer. The account identifier reached us in floating-point notation with six significant digits, which is how a spreadsheet writes a nineteen-digit integer. The 35 values are distinct at that precision, but a full export written the same way could not be relied on to distinguish accounts. The sample itself carries real reading times, so it is deposited for peer review under embargo until Stage 2 acceptance, at the location the Data availability statement names.
