Text properties and children’s reading pace in a multilingual storytelling app

Abstract

Children increasingly read machine-generated stories on personal devices, and it is not known whether the text properties that govern reading under controlled conditions govern it there. In this Registered Report, we will analyse behavioural logs from Let’s Story, a storytelling feature of the Applaydu app, in which children aged 7 and older read in ten or more languages. We will model reading time per word from each text’s lexical difficulty, grammatical load and referential continuity, computed with language-general tools. Six confirmatory hypotheses concern these associations, their moderation by age band, the selectivity of read-aloud narration, children’s own difficulty ratings and accumulated in-app reading. Bayesian crossed-effects models will represent readings within texts, children and languages, and every association will be judged against a region of practical equivalence. Because narration, text choice and continued use are chosen by families, we will report every coefficient as a conditional association.

1 Introduction

A generation of children now meets a large share of its reading on a screen, and an increasing share of what appears there was written by a machine. Commercial storytelling applications generate a narrative to order from a handful of choices a family makes, read it aloud in the family’s language, and record what the child did with it. The behavioural record such platforms accumulate is orders of magnitude larger than any laboratory corpus of children’s reading, spans dozens of languages, and arises under exactly the conditions that laboratory work has to strip away. It is also almost entirely unexamined. We ask whether the text properties that govern reading under controlled conditions still govern it in the wild, and whether their hold on the reader changes over the years in which decoding becomes automatic.

The laboratory answer is well established at three levels of linguistic description. At the level of the word, frequency of occurrence is among the most robust predictors of reading behaviour that psycholinguistics has produced. High-frequency words are recognised faster and fixated for less time than low-frequency words, across dozens of eye-tracking studies and with remarkable consistency across languages and orthographies (Mézière et al., 2023; Norman et al., 2025). In children the effect tends to be larger than in adults, reflecting less automatised lexical access (Hsiao & Nation, 2018). Frequency norms drawn from subtitle corpora approximate everyday exposure well enough to have become the standard operationalisation (Brysbaert et al., 2019; van Heuven et al., 2014). Resources built from what children actually read, such as the Children and Young People’s Books Lexicon, give a still more defensible baseline for developing readers (Korochkina et al., 2024). Related lexical properties behave similarly. Concrete and highly imageable words are processed faster and remembered better than abstract ones (Magnabosco & Hauk, 2024), an advantage usually attributed to the availability of perceptual as well as verbal representations (Bernabeu, 2022; Kaup et al., 2025). The advantage is pronounced in younger readers, who lean more heavily on grounded representations when background knowledge runs out (Muraki et al., 2025). The diversity of the vocabulary a text deploys cuts differently according to who is reading: varied word choice may sustain a skilled adult, whereas for a child still spending resources on decoding it can overwhelm them (Cain & Oakhill, 2014; Fredrick & Craven, 2025; Van den Bosch et al., 2019).

At the level of the sentence, structure imposes its own cost. Mean dependency distance, the count of embedded clauses and the ratio of subordinate to main clauses all index the demand a text places on the reader to hold a dependent in mind until its head arrives (Nielsen et al., 2025). Long dependencies are expensive because the intervening material has to be carried, and because children’s verbal working memory is still developing they are disproportionately affected by that cost relative to adults (Chang, 2020; Cui et al., 2024; Delage & Frauenfelder, 2019). Sensitivity to grammatical structure continues to underwrite comprehension well past the primary years: Marjokorpi and van Rijt (2025) found explicit grammatical understanding to predict reading comprehension in secondary-school students after socioeconomic background, writing ability and other literacy factors were controlled.

At the level of the passage, what matters is whether the text states the connection between one idea and the next or leaves the reader to supply it. Sentence-to-sentence semantic similarity, overlap in referring expressions and the density of explicit connectives together index how much of that work the surface of the text has already done (Crossley et al., 2019). Texts that do more of it are generally easier to follow, and the benefit is largest for readers with least prior knowledge to draw on (Hattan & Kendeou, 2024). Automated tools now make these indices computable at scale (Crossley, 2025; Graesser et al., 2014), and multi-dimensional characterisations of difficulty built from them outperform the classical readability formulae they replaced (Crossley et al., 2023; Goldman, 2024; Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

The systematic review that accompanies this protocol sharpens that general account in ways that shape the present design. Text difficulty does not reduce to word frequency: in 924 German fourth-graders, estimated lexical coverage predicted comprehension non-linearly, rising more steeply above roughly 56 per cent coverage (Ludewig et al., 2023). In 158 children aged between 8 years 8 months and 11 years 2 months, word frequency, concreteness, deep cohesion, narrativity and lexical arousal each explained unique variance in passage comprehension after decoding and oral language were taken into account (Pickren et al., 2022), which is the direct warrant for carrying affective and discourse variables alongside conventional readability measures. Effects also depend on what is being asked: across 1,229 children in grades 2 to 9, the direction and size of a text characteristic’s contribution varied with question type (Aparicio et al., 2022). Feature-based prediction of item-level comprehension from sentence complexity, lexical surprisal and rare-word use is already feasible (Nie et al., 2022), and syntactic complexity has been shown to impair comprehension accuracy and slow recall in bilingual children (Wasito Kirana et al., 2025).

Two further strands bear on the present design because they concern the reader and the reading context. The first is the child’s history of reading. Longitudinal work is unambiguous that this history cannot be read as a simple dose of practice. Using random-intercept cross-lagged models from ages 7 to 16, Torppa et al. (2020) found that weaker early reading predicted less subsequent leisure reading, while later within-person increases in book reading predicted gains in comprehension. A ten-year study likewise found reciprocal associations between skill and print exposure, with the skill-to-practice path most visible early and accumulated exposure becoming more predictive later (van Bergen et al., 2021). Accumulated use of an app therefore carries both practice and the stable differences in skill and motivation that produced the use in the first place, so we will estimate within-child change and between-child differences separately.

The second is narration. Read-aloud audio is often treated as a uniform benefit, and the evidence does not support that reading. A meta-analysis of 30 reading-while-listening studies (62 effects, total N = 1,945) found a small pooled comprehension advantage (\(g\) = 0.18), no reliable advantage where ordinary reading was self-paced (\(g\) = 0.06, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.19]), and a larger one only where ordinary reading was paced by the experimenter (\(g\) = 0.41, 95% CI [0.13, 0.70]) (Clinton-Lisell, 2023). Among students with reading difficulties, text-to-speech tools showed a moderate pooled effect (\(d\) = 0.35) that fell to \(d\) = 0.15 and included zero in within-participant designs (Wood et al., 2018). Neither synthesis settles the mechanism, which a large naturalistic corpus is well placed to address. If a spoken rendition works by supplying word identity without print-to-sound mapping, it should remove much of the cost that lexical difficulty imposes while leaving the cost of connecting ideas largely intact. If narrated reading time is instead set by the duration of the recording, every text property should lose its purchase on time in the same proportion. Hypothesis 4 is written to separate these accounts.

The absence of such analyses can be counted. On 26 August 2026, a Scopus search over 2018 to 2026 returned 3,701 records on children’s reading comprehension, 6,592 on computational text features and 133,132 on platform log data. The pairwise intersections were 17, 28 and 45 records. The three-way intersection was empty. The exact expressions are reproduced in the supplementary search record, the script that issues them and archives their counts is deposited with this protocol (Bernabeu, 2026c), and Figure 1 shows the sets. Over the same window, records combining children’s reading with machine-generated text went from none before 2024 to two, six and four in the three years since. The material children read is changing faster than the evidence base that describes it.

Let’s Story, a feature of the Applaydu app developed by Gameloft and Ferrero International, sits in that empty intersection. Families choose a setting, a hero, a companion, a plot and a theme. A generative model then writes a narrative around those choices, and the app renders it as an illustrated storybook and, at the family’s option, reads it aloud (Ratner et al., 2025). Every completed reading is logged, with the time taken, the language, the child’s age band, the reading level the family has set and an optional judgement of how hard the story was. Comparable platforms have been studied one feature at a time. Booton et al. (2023) reviewed 11 studies and found read-aloud narration to aid comprehension while additional interactive games did not. Content analyses of the wider market are less encouraging: Furlong et al. (2025) found expert reviewers willing to recommend only 27.5 per cent of 309 phonics apps, and Chatzopoulos et al. (2023) found educational content the weakest dimension of 50 Google Play apps and consumer star ratings a poor guide to expert quality. Log data from a research-oriented platform have been shown to track ability, with reading speed in the BookBites app correlating with standardised scores at r = .39 to .48 (Sortkær et al., 2021), but the commercial edutainment sector, whose user base is far larger, has not been examined this way at all.

The theoretical commitments behind the hypotheses are held in a machine-checkable theory object, deposited with this protocol, that names twelve constructs, eighteen propositions each carrying the mechanism it claims, six auxiliary assumptions and six rival accounts, and records which prediction is derived from which proposition and which rival it is meant to separate. Figure 2 is generated from that object, so the figure and the hypotheses cannot drift apart. The object passes all twelve items of the versioned rigour checklist implemented in theoryforge, scoring 90.9 out of 100 (Supplementary Section S1) (Bernabeu, 2026d). Its causal graph is acyclic, so implications can be derived from it mechanically: that the three text properties are mutually independent given how a text came to be written, that narration is unrelated to accumulated reading, and that the difficulty rating adds nothing about reading level once reading time is known. We will test these implications before interpreting the confirmatory models, and they can fail.

2 The evidential status of naturalistic platform data

This study is observational throughout. Children choose their stories, their reading level and whether to switch narration on, and families choose whether to keep using the app. A protocol built on such records has to say what that buys and what it costs, because the answer is not that platform data are a cheaper substitute for an experiment. They are a different instrument, and they answer a different question. A laboratory experiment establishes that a text property can move reading when an experimenter controls the text, the setting and the task. It cannot establish that the property does move reading when nobody controls anything. The second question is one about generalisation, and it cannot be settled in the setting that produced the original result (Nastase et al., 2020; Yarkoni, 2022). Explanation and prediction are best treated as complementary aims served by different designs (Hofman et al., 2017; Hofman et al., 2021), and the same holds for control and representativeness. Mining naturally occurring records is by now an established methodology with its own accumulated cautions (Goldstone & Lupyan, 2016; Paxton & Griffiths, 2017), and the account below draws on them.

The first thing the platform buys is stimulus sampling. Reading research has known since Clark (1973) that treating a handful of experimenter-written texts as a fixed set inflates confidence in conclusions meant to hold for texts in general, and the mixed-model solution has been available for over a decade (Judd et al., 2012). Yarkoni’s (2022) survey of the problem found that psychologists routinely model variation between participants while leaving stimuli, tasks and sites unmodelled, which constrains generalisation far more tightly than most reports acknowledge. The design here treats Text as a crossed random factor over hundreds of texts that children selected for themselves, which is both the prescribed remedy and, in Brunswik’s sense, a representative design as opposed to a systematic one: the stimuli are sampled from the environment the reader inhabits, not constructed to instantiate a factorial contrast (Dhami et al., 2004). We avoid the phrase ecological validity throughout, since it has been shown to carry several incompatible meanings in current use (Holleman et al., 2020; Kihlstrom, 2021). The claim made here is the narrower and checkable one about how the stimuli were sampled. The precision analysis makes the same point in reverse, since it is the number of distinct texts, not the number of sessions, that bounds every text coefficient.

Linguistic breadth is the second gain. English dominates cognitive science to a degree that warps which mechanisms are studied and licenses overgeneralisation from one atypical language to the species (Blasi et al., 2022). Reading is among the worst affected, because writing systems differ in ways that matter for every stage of processing. The most ambitious response to date, the Multilingual Eye-movement Corpus, assembled eye-tracking during text reading in 13 languages through an international multi-laboratory collaboration, and its authors open by reporting that the existing empirical base is insufficient for cross-linguistic comparison (Siegelman et al., 2022). A commercial platform reaches a comparable number of languages incidentally, in numbers of readers no consortium could fund. The comparison must be stated precisely, because MECO records eye movements and this study records only whole-story duration. The platform buys breadth at the cost of resolution, and it is worth being clear which of the two a given question needs.

The third gain is behaviour as it occurred. Where the alternative measure of how much or how fast a child reads is an adult’s recollection, the log is the better instrument: logged and self-reported digital media use diverge systematically enough to have warranted a meta-analysis of the discrepancy (Parry et al., 2021).

Against this stands the sharpest statistical objection to any large non-random dataset. Meng’s (2018) decomposition shows that the error of a sample mean is the product of three quantities. One indexes data quality, as the correlation between the recorded value and whether it was recorded at all. The other two index data quantity and the difficulty of the problem. Probability sampling holds the first near zero. Once it is not held there, the population size stops cancelling: estimation error then grows with the square root of the population. Bradley and colleagues (2021) gave the canonical demonstration: two surveys collecting roughly 250,000 and 75,000 responses overestimated United States COVID-19 vaccine uptake by 17 and 14 percentage points, with margins of error so small that the wrong answers looked precise, while a well-designed panel of about 1,000 responses was accurate. Scale converts a small bias into a confident error, and it did so at Google before it did so in the pandemic (Lazer et al., 2014). Every selection gate in this study is a candidate for that correlation: who owns a tablet, who installs an app tied to a confectionery brand, who registers consent for research use, and who finishes a story.

That objection is real, and it also has a boundary, which must be drawn accurately, not merely seized on. Meng’s result concerns the estimation of a population mean, and this study estimates no population mean. Its estimands are conditional associations inside the sample, such as the change in log reading time per word for a one-standard-deviation change in lexical difficulty, within a language and within a reading level. A sample that is unrepresentative in who it contains biases such a slope only when selection depends on the outcome given the predictors, which is a narrower condition than the one that wrecks a mean. The consequence falls on the claim, not on the analysis: we will report these coefficients as estimates for children who read on this platform, and we will not present them as population values for children in general, nor will we report a population mean of reading speed for any group.

One selection gate does meet the narrower condition, and it deserves separate treatment. The extract contains completed readings only, so the analysis conditions on completion, which is plausibly a common effect of the text’s difficulty and the reader’s capacity. Conditioning on a common effect can induce an association between its causes where none existed (Rohrer, 2018). The direction here is predictable: if harder texts are abandoned more often, the hard texts that survive into the data are disproportionately those read by children who could manage them, which attenuates the very coefficients Hypothesis 1 predicts. The bias therefore works against our own prediction, so a non-negligible result survives the objection more comfortably than a null does, and the practical consequence is that an inside-region verdict for Hypotheses 1 to 3 must be reported with the completion rate beside it. That rate is question (k) to the data controller.

Measurement error in digital traces has its own taxonomy, and the errors specific to platform data are not the errors of surveys (Sen et al., 2021). Four apply here. The platform, not the investigator, defines the categories, so age band and reading level are its constructs. The instrument drifts, because the app changes version across the window. The interval that the timing column covers is not fully specified, which is question (c). And a session timeout would right-censor the outcome, which is why the censoring rule is registered in advance. None of these is fatal, and each is one of the reasons the audit precedes the models.

Nothing here is randomised, and the protocol does not use causal verbs for variables that families chose. That restraint is not the same as pretending the questions are not causal: euphemism has been argued to damage observational research by obscuring what is actually being asked (Grosz et al., 2020; Hernán, 2018). The position taken is to state the causal structure explicitly in a deposited graph, derive the adjustment set from it, register the rival explanations it implies, and then report associations conditional on that adjustment. Estimating the text coefficients within reading level, for instance, is a consequence of the graph, not a precaution added to it.

Secondary data also invite the analytic flexibility that preregistration exists to remove, and the field has developed specific standards for preregistering analyses of data that already exist (Van den Akker et al., 2021; Weston et al., 2019). The arrangement described under Data provenance goes further on the point that matters most. The extract is unanalysed because it is absent: the data controller holds it, and it reaches us only after in-principle acceptance. The most common objection to observational work, that the analyst saw the data before choosing the analysis, is answered here by construction.

Finally, scale should not be mistaken for understanding. When 160 teams applied machine learning to a rich birth-cohort dataset to predict six life outcomes, the best predictions were barely better than a simple benchmark (Salganik et al., 2020). This study accordingly makes a deliberately narrow claim. It does not attempt to predict a child’s reading, but to estimate a handful of pre-specified associations whose attainable precision was calculated before the data were released, and to say in advance which of them the available records cannot settle.

A last limit applies to any aggregate. Group-level associations need not describe any individual, and variance around an expected value has been found to be two to four times larger within people than within groups (Fisher et al., 2018). This is why tier 2 separates each child’s own change from the differences between children, and why the two are never reported as a single quantity.

Three consequences of all this are built into the design. First, the reading level a family sets determines which version of a text the platform serves, so text difficulty is partly assigned by a choice that also reflects the child’s capacity, and we will therefore estimate the text coefficients within reading level and check whether they survive that restriction. Second, the app records elapsed time for a whole story and never for a page, so the outcome is a rate, and we state below which analyses that forecloses. Third, the data carry no measure of any individual child’s reading skill, vocabulary or language background, so age band stands in for a bundle of developing capacities and is interpreted as nothing finer.

Six confirmatory hypotheses follow, set out with their models, decision rules and interpretations in Table 1. Two of them, the interactions between the text properties and the age band, are registered as two-sided tests that adjudicate between rival accounts, because the direct developmental evidence in this age range supports neither sign. Each account, and the reading of every possible outcome, is given where the hypotheses are stated.

3 Hypotheses

Three text components carry the confirmatory predictions. LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad are oriented so that higher scores mean a more demanding text, and ReferentialContinuity so that higher scores mean a text that states more of the links between its ideas. Signs are fixed before any component score is joined to an outcome. The age band is coded 0 for children recorded as 7 to 8 and 1 for children recorded as 9 or older, and narration is coded 0 when the spoken rendition was off and 1 when it was on. The outcome for Hypotheses 1, 2, 3 and 6 is log reading time per word, standardised within language, so a coefficient is a within-language standardised slope and the region of practical equivalence is 0.05 standard deviations. Hypothesis 4 concerns a difference between two attenuation proportions, with a region of 0.05 on that difference. Hypothesis 5 uses the child’s own three-point difficulty rating on an ordinal scale, where the region is an odds ratio between 0.95 and 1.05.

Because the two main effects run in opposite directions, the same developmental claim takes opposite signs in the two interaction terms. Grammatical load slows reading, so a weaker effect in older children makes a positive slope smaller and the interaction negative. Referential continuity speeds reading, so a weaker effect in older children makes a negative slope less negative and the interaction positive. Wherever an account is described below as predicting that a text property bears less on the older reader, the sign of the corresponding coefficient follows from this convention alone.

Two features of the outcome do work that the hypotheses depend on, and both are fixed here. First, children in the two age bands differ in overall reading rate, and a difference in rate that is purely multiplicative manufactures a spurious interaction on a raw time scale (Faust et al., 1999). The outcome is therefore the logarithm of reading time throughout, on which a multiplicative difference in rate appears as a main effect and never as an interaction, so that every interaction reported here is a difference in proportional cost. Second, in the standard cohesion manipulation the more cohesive version of a text is 20 to 40 per cent longer, so a duration aggregated over a whole text would show higher coherence slowing reading for arithmetic reasons alone. Dividing by word count and retaining log text length as a covariate closes that path, and a raw duration is never used as an outcome.

A hypothesis is declared untestable, before its model is fitted and without reference to any coefficient, if its component fails the construction rule set out under the dimension reduction or if its outcome or contrast is absent from the frozen extract. A hypothesis whose data fall below the precision threshold recorded for it in Table 1 is instead declared underpowered: its model is still fitted and reported, and no conclusion is drawn from an interval the design analysis predicted would be indeterminate.

Table 1 sets out each hypothesis against the sampling and analysis plan that tests it, the precision the design analysis says that test requires, how each possible outcome will be read, and what would be shown wrong. Every row was fixed before any outcome data existed.

3.1 Hypothesis 1: text properties and reading pace

We predict that, across distinct texts and within reading level and language, LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad will each show a positive association with log reading time per word, and ReferentialContinuity a negative one. The three coefficients are evaluated and labelled separately. An overall verdict on Hypothesis 1 is recorded only when all three are in the predicted direction and practically non-negligible, and the failure of one does not suppress reporting of the others.

3.2 Hypothesis 2: grammatical load and the age band

We register the GrammaticalLoad × AgeBand interaction as a two-sided adjudicating test, because two accounts of what changes between these ages predict opposite signs on the scale this study uses.

On the capacity account, a grammatical dependency costs time in proportion to how close its maintenance comes to exhausting the working memory available, so as that capacity matures the same structure costs proportionally less and the interaction is negative (Delage & Frauenfelder, 2019; Peng et al., 2018). On the experience account, what changes is not capacity but exposure: an older reader has met complex structure far more often and has been taught the punctuation that marks its boundaries, engages with it more fully in consequence, and pays a proportionally larger cost, which makes the interaction positive (MacDonald & Christiansen, 2002; Montag & MacDonald, 2015).

The direct evidence is open. The best-powered test over this exact band found no attenuation at all, but it manipulated temporary ambiguity, which indexes reanalysis, not the sustained maintenance that dependency length imposes, so its null does not transfer to the present operationalisation (Wonnacott et al., 2016). Where an interaction has been reported it was confined to late measures with fourteen children per group, and in that same study the complexity effect expressed as a proportion of baseline grew with age, from 15 per cent in the younger children to 25 per cent in the older and 31 per cent in adults (Joseph & Liversedge, 2013). Since the outcome here is log reading time per word, its coefficients are proportional, and that growth is exactly the pattern a positive interaction would reproduce. A third possibility is that development over this band is not monotonic at all (Booth et al., 2000).

A negative interaction outside the region of practical equivalence supports the capacity account, a positive one supports the experience account, and a posterior lying inside the region is evidence that neither developmental signature reaches a detectable size across these two bands. That third outcome would still be informative: no existing test had more than 81 children.

3.3 Hypothesis 3: referential continuity and the age band

We register the ReferentialContinuity × AgeBand interaction the same way, because the construction-integration framework supplies two moderators of coherence facilitation that both rise between 7 and 11 and push in opposite directions.

On the knowledge account, a reader who cannot bridge a gap from what they already know depends on the text to state the connection, so surface continuity is worth most to the younger reader and the interaction is positive, the facilitation being a negative slope that becomes less negative with age. On the cue-utilisation account, exploiting a connective requires enough decoding fluency to reach it and enough explicit knowledge of what it signals, so the older reader gains more and the interaction is negative (O’reilly & Mcnamara, 2007).

Here too the existing evidence does not decide between the accounts. Eight- and ten-year-olds gained the same speed-up from an appropriate connective in self-paced reading, with the age interaction explicitly null (Cain & Nash, 2011). Online sensitivity to a coherence break did not differ between children of 8 to 9 and children of 10 to 11 (Helder et al., 2016). The single demonstration of a reverse cohesion effect in children turned on world knowledge within a single grade, and neither age nor decoding skill played any part in it. No study has manipulated whole-text coherence in this age range and measured reading rate, so that cell of the literature is empty.

The three outcomes are read as for Hypothesis 2. Because the sign of a coherence effect also depends on which cohesive device carries it, connectives enter by relation type and the component’s anchors are the causal and contrastive connectives, for the reason given under the dimension reduction.

3.4 Hypothesis 4: the selectivity of narration

We predict that narration attenuates the LexicalDifficulty coefficient proportionally more than the ReferentialContinuity coefficient. Writing \(\pi_{\text{lex}}\) and \(\pi_{\text{cont}}\) for the proportional attenuation of each coefficient under narration, computed draw by draw from the posterior, the prediction is that \(\pi_{\text{lex}} - \pi_{\text{cont}} > 0.20\). A spoken rendition supplies word identity without print-to-sound mapping, which is the step that a low-frequency or long word makes expensive, and it does not supply the link between two ideas, which the reader must still construct. Reading aloud only the decoding-heavy elements of a test helps poor decoders while a pacing-only control does nothing (Giusto & Ehri, 2019). The benefit is confined to children whose decoding is weak but whose language is intact (Keelor et al., 2023), and within a narrated passage the time saved falls on the first encounter with an orthographically novel word and has gone by the second (Valentini et al., 2024).

The competing account holds that narrated reading time is set by the duration of the recording, in which case every text coefficient shrinks by the same proportion and \(\pi_{\text{lex}} - \pi_{\text{cont}} = 0\). That account has substantial support of its own. The aggregate comprehension benefit of reading while listening is confined to studies whose comparison condition was externally paced (Clinton-Lisell, 2022; Clinton-Lisell, 2023). A preregistered test of the resource-freeing mechanism found comprehension worse under narration, and attributed it to a forced pace blocking rereading (Hui & Godfroid, 2026). Where the compression has been measured incidentally it is severe: the standard deviation of exposure time fell from 11.56 seconds when self-paced to 0.62 seconds when narrated (Valentini et al., 2024). No study has yet regressed reading time on decoding-level and on integration-level text predictors separately, in narrated and unnarrated reading of the same texts, so neither account has been tested on the contrast that would separate them.

Two safeguards follow. First, the model will estimate a separate residual standard deviation for narrated and for unnarrated sessions, and we will report that ratio alongside every Hypothesis 4 estimate. A large fall in residual variability under narration is the signature of a recording setting the pace, and we will not interpret the selectivity contrast without it. Second, we will repeat the contrast in the subset of narrated sessions in which the audio cannot have been binding, meaning those read faster than 160 words per minute, a rate below the slowest adult silent reading rate recorded across languages and therefore a conservative ceiling on any narration rate. If the selectivity survives where the recording was not the constraint, pacing does not explain it. Should the data controller supply the audio duration, that bound will be replaced by the duration itself and we will report the substitution as a deviation from this protocol.

Because the contrast is a ratio, we will evaluate it only when the lexical and continuity coefficients from Hypothesis 1 are both practically non-negligible in sessions without narration. If either is not, there is no attenuation to compare and Hypothesis 4 is reported as untestable. This gating rule does not depend on the sign of any coefficient.

3.5 Hypothesis 5: the child’s own judgement of difficulty

We predict that, in an ordinal model of the post-reading rating, LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad will raise the odds of a harder rating and ReferentialContinuity will lower them, each by an odds ratio outside 0.95 to 1.05, and that the three standardised coefficients will rank by magnitude as they do in the reading-time model. The second clause makes this more than Hypothesis 1 repeated on a second outcome. Two measures of the same three constructs, one behavioural and one introspective, obtained on different scales from overlapping sets of children, should rank those constructs the same way, and we will report the posterior probability that they do as the study’s principal convergent-validity check.

Two facts about the rating constrain the design, and neither is established in the literature. No published study has had children of this age give a global post-reading judgement of how difficult a text was and validated it against the text’s measured properties: the child metacognition literature rates adjacent things, such as confidence in one’s own answers, judgements of learning for word lists and enjoyment of an activity. The three-point easy, just right and difficult format is a classroom heuristic with no published psychometric validation, and nothing licenses assuming that a single rating from a single child is reliable. That children can validly judge text difficulty is therefore something this study tests, not something it assumes.

For the design, the effective sample size is the number of distinct children rating each text, and the raw count of ratings overstates it, so the model carries a text varying intercept and we will report the distribution of raters per text before fitting. A text rated by fewer than five distinct children does not contribute. We will report the intraclass correlation for the rating at text level, the reliability estimate the literature does not supply.

For interpretation, a failure here is ambiguous on its own, and Hypothesis 1 resolves it. If the text components predict reading time but not the rating, the feature pipeline is sound and the rating is not a usable measure of experienced difficulty, which is worth reporting in its own right given how widely such single-item checks are deployed in educational software. If they predict neither, the components themselves are suspect. If they predict both and in the same order, two independent measurements converge on the same ordering of text demands. All three readings are recorded here in advance.

3.6 Hypothesis 6: accumulated reading and pace

We predict that, among children with several recorded sessions, a doubling of a child’s own accumulated in-app reading will be followed by a reduction in that child’s log reading time per word, over and above session order and the interval since the previous session. Every encounter with a written word form sharpens the representation supporting its later recognition, so reading consolidates the machinery that makes reading cheap. The relation between practice and skill is reciprocal over years (Torppa et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2021), so the coefficient is an association within the observation window, not an estimate of a practice effect. This hypothesis depends on a subsample whose size is not known before the extract is released, and it is declared underpowered, not untestable, if that subsample falls below the threshold in Table 1.

Every hypothesis is labelled under the single posterior rule given under Decision rules and diagnostics, and an inconclusive result is reported as such. No hypothesis is filtered by a preliminary significance test. The one registered exception is the Hypothesis 4 gating rule above, under which that hypothesis is reported as untestable.

4.1 Ethics

Ethical approval for secondary analysis of these anonymised records was granted by the Departmental Research Ethics Committee of the Department of Education, University of Oxford (reference [to be inserted]). 4.2 Data provenance, sequestration and outcome blindness

The data this protocol analyses already exist. Nature Human Behaviour admits secondary analyses of existing data on condition that authors can evidence no prior access to the data in question, and the arrangement set out below was put to the journal’s editorial office and confirmed as appropriate before this protocol was written.

The data controller builds the export now, on its own release cycle, and holds it. Nothing of the session-level file reaches us until the journal confirms in-principle acceptance in writing. In the interval we receive three things, each of which the editorial office agreed may be received in advance. The first is a manifest: the column list, the row count, the cut-off timestamp, the build date and a SHA-256 checksum for every file, so that the file eventually transferred can be shown to be the file that was built. The second is the story texts, keyed by story identifier, reading language, reading level and app version, with page breaks preserved, because every linguistic measure in this protocol is computed on them and none of them contains a behavioural outcome. The third is a set of aggregate counts computed by the data controller from the export by running a script we supplied, which reads ten columns, writes one small table of counts, and never reports a reading time as a value.

The counts deserve detail, because a count computed from an outcome column is where blindness would fail. The script treats reading time only as a column whose usability has to be established. It reports how many values are absent, non-numeric, negative or zero, and how many fall into each of seven bands whose boundaries are successive powers of ten, together with the number of distinct values the column holds and the largest number of sessions sharing any single value. It never crosses reading time with language, age, text, account or date. Bands ten times wider than one another cannot distinguish a fast text from a slow one. What they can distinguish is a column recorded in seconds from one recorded in milliseconds, which the analysis has to know before it can be specified at all. The count of sessions sharing one value is how a session-timeout cap makes itself visible, and the sampling plan below turns that count into a registered decision about censoring.

Two disclosures follow. First, between 27 July and 6 August 2026 we received a 35-row structural sample and the data dictionary, shared to confirm the shape of the table, together with the summary described above computed on that sample. The sample carries real reading times for 35 sessions on five distinct texts, one session per account. It cannot support any hypothesis in this protocol: none is identified by 35 observations, and every within-child term requires accounts with more than one session, of which it contains none. It is nonetheless part of the record, and we report it here. Second, at the editorial office’s specific direction, we have not run the dimension reduction specified below and will not run it before submission, although we hold the texts on which it would operate. The component solution is therefore fully specified here and entirely unfitted.

The data controller will supply a signed certification naming an owner and a deputy for the held file, and stating its build date, its window, its row count and its checksums. The same document certifies three things about what has happened to the file: that it has not been transferred in whole or in part to the research team or to anyone acting for it, that it will be transferred only on written confirmation of in-principle acceptance, and that no analysis relating story text to reading time has been conducted by the controller or reported to the team. It is reproduced in the supplementary material.

4.3 Design and sampling plan

The design is observational and the sample is a census: we will analyse every eligible record in one immutable extract, whose closing date is fixed before any outcome model is fitted, with no sequential testing and no optional stopping.

Two nested samples serve different hypotheses, because those hypotheses are identified at different levels and the data supporting them are unequally certain.

Tier 1 carries the text hypotheses. It comprises every completed reading in the extract for which the account is recorded in age band 7-8 or 9 and over, the reading language is one the text-feature pipeline covers, the story text has been supplied, and the reading time falls inside the plausibility bounds below. Where an account read the same text more than once, only the first reading enters tier 1, and repeated readings are retained for the practice analysis described under sensitivity. The unit of analysis is one reading of one text by one account.

Whether tier 1 can estimate a child varying intercept depends on how many accounts read more than one distinct text, which is not known in advance. If at least ten per cent of tier-1 readings come from accounts contributing two or more readings, the model includes a varying intercept for Account. If fewer do, that term is not separable from residual variance: it is dropped, child variance is carried in the residual, and the reduction in the effective independence of the sample is stated wherever a tier-1 interval is reported. We will read the proportion from the provider-computed counts before fitting anything.

Tier 2 carries the within-child hypothesis. It comprises those tier-1 accounts contributing at least three eligible readings of distinct texts. Three is the smallest number that leaves a degree of freedom once a within-child slope and an intercept are estimated, and the threshold is varied to two and to five in the sensitivity analyses. Tier 2 estimates a child varying intercept and separates each child’s mean level of a predictor from that reading’s deviation from it, so a within-child association is not conflated with stable differences between children who habitually read at different rates. How many accounts reach the tier-2 threshold is not known in advance. The structural sample contains none, but it holds one session per account by construction and cannot speak to the full window.

Reading time enters as elapsed seconds for the whole story divided by the story’s word count, and we bound it by what a child could physically be doing, not by anything observed in these data. We will exclude a reading faster than 400 words per minute, or 0.15 seconds per word, as faster than reading. We will exclude a reading slower than 10 words per minute, or six seconds per word, as a story left open. The upper bound follows the only published precedent that states such a rule and its yield: a meta-analysis of Spanish reading rates deleted silent rates above 400 words per minute as exceeding what a competent adult can manage and as indicating skipped text, removing 313 of 19,237 cases (Ripoll Salceda et al., 2020). The lower bound sits far below the slowest group mean in that literature, 30 words per minute for Spanish first-graders reading silently, so it cannot exclude a merely slow reader. Both bounds are wide by design, so the primary analysis removes only the impossible. Tighter treatments are sensitivity analyses. We will report the number and proportion removed by every rule, by language, age band, reading level and narration state.

One further rule concerns the top of the distribution. Where a session timeout caps the recorded interval, the cap shows itself as an implausible number of sessions sharing one value, which is one of the quantities the provider-computed counts report. If those counts show more than one per cent of sessions sharing a single value, we will locate that value in the transferred extract before fitting any outcome model. If it lies in the upper decile of the reading-time distribution, we will treat it as a right-censoring point and fit the timing models as censored lognormal models, with those observations censored, not discarded.

Ahead of all of the above, we will exclude sessions the platform flags as incomplete or crashed, duplicated event records, accounts flagged as duplicates under the platform’s own data-quality rules, and stories without a positive word count. A participant-flow diagram will report every exclusion and the retained accounts, readings and texts, overall and by language.

4.4 Materials

The reading materials are the story texts themselves. A family chooses a setting, a hero, a companion, a plot and a theme, and a generative model writes a narrative around that combination. The text is then fixed, and it is not rewritten when the same combination is chosen again. Texts also exist in a fixed classic set that families may read instead of a generated story. Alongside the texts, the extract records which of the two a reading drew on, the reading level the platform was serving, the language, the app version and the page count.

For identification, a text is a stimulus shared by many readers, not a property of a session, and every text coefficient in this protocol is identified by comparing texts. How many distinct texts the extract holds is therefore the single quantity that governs how much this design can establish, and it is not yet known: the data controller’s own estimate spans a few hundred to a few million, according to whether one combination of chosen attributes maps to one text shared by everyone who picks it or to a separate text per session. The design analysis below is built around that uncertainty, and Table 2 records what follows from each answer.

For every text, we will compute features in four families. Lexical features are mean log frequency of content words, the proportion of words with a frequency below one per million tokens on the uniform frequency resource, mean rated age of acquisition, mean orthographic length, mean per-token surprisal under a language-specific neural language model, lexical density, the Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity and concreteness. Grammatical features are mean dependency distance from a universal-dependencies parse, clauses per sentence, the ratio of subordinate to main clauses, mean sentence length and mean depth of noun-phrase modification. Discourse features are mean semantic similarity between adjacent sentences from a multilingual sentence encoder, the proportion of adjacent sentence pairs sharing a referring expression, connectives per hundred words by relation type and narrativity. Affective features are the mean and the variability of valence and of arousal.

Supplementary Table 3 names the resource used for each feature in each language, with its coverage. Four rules govern that table and are fixed before any feature is computed. The first addresses a problem specific to multilingual work and unsolved in the literature: a variable measured by a different instrument in each language confounds the method of measurement with the language, so that a language-level slope no longer means the same thing twice over. Each feature is therefore computed from a single resource applied identically in every language, even where a better native resource exists in one of them, and native norms are run as a pre-specified sensitivity analysis with the per-language correlation between the two reported. Second, no value is ever carried across languages by translating a word list, and a predicted resource is admitted only where the prediction was made within each language by one documented procedure and its correlation with native ratings is reported. Where no acceptable measure exists for a language, that language does not contribute that feature. Third, every measure reports the proportion of tokens it matched, and a language in which a measure matches fewer than 80 per cent of tokens does not contribute it. A separate per-text rule under the dimension reduction governs which texts enter the component solution. Fourth, the full inventory, the software versions, the model checkpoints, the tokenisation rules and the matched-token proportions will be frozen and published before any feature is joined to an outcome. Korean is the language these rules constrain most, and the consequences for it are set out beside that table.

4.5 Measures

The processing outcome, serving Hypotheses 1 to 4 and 6, is the natural logarithm of elapsed reading time divided by the story’s word count, standardised within reading language. We describe it throughout as reading pace or processing time, never as a measure of comprehension or fluency, because nothing in these data licenses that reading. The outcome for Hypothesis 5 is the child’s optional post-reading rating of the story as easy, just right or difficult, treated as ordered and modelled on a cumulative-logit scale.

The confirmatory predictors are the three component scores defined below. Text length in words, narrativity, mean valence and mean arousal are pre-specified secondary text predictors and enter every model as covariates. The variability of valence and of arousal, and the additive and temporal connective densities, are computed for description and enter no confirmatory model. Whether the text was generated or drawn from the classic set enters as a covariate, and the substantive comparison between the two is a contingent hypothesis, for reasons given under Table 2.

Three variables describe the reader and the reading context. The age band is the platform’s own record of the child’s age group, reduced to the two bands this study covers. The reading level is the level the family has set for the session, which determines which version of a text is served and is therefore an attribute of the reading as well as of the reader. Narration is the session-level flag recording that a spoken rendition accompanied the reading. All three are chosen by the family and none is randomised.

Accumulated in-app reading, which serves Hypothesis 6, is the cumulative count of words in completed distinct stories the account read before the current session, on a log scale. Session order and the number of days since the account’s previous eligible session enter alongside it, so that accumulated reading is separated from the simple passage of time and from the position of a session in a sequence. Within tier 2 the measure is decomposed into the account’s own mean and the current session’s deviation from it, and the deviation term is the registered test.

The data are crossed, not nested: readings sit within accounts, a text is met by many accounts and exists in several languages and at several reading levels, and an account may read in more than one language. We will therefore treat Account, Text, Language, Country and App version as crossed grouping factors wherever replication permits, and report the counts supporting each before fitting any model. Reading level, with only three levels, enters as a fixed factor. If at least 90 per cent of texts are read in only one eligible session, a Text varying intercept is not estimable and is replaced by varying intercepts for the repeated attribute combination that generated the text. That rule and any consequence of it will be reported.

4.6 Measurement, availability and missingness audit

Before fitting any outcome model, we will run and archive an audit of the frozen extract and its dictionary. It establishes field availability and units, the granularity of the age field, how often a text and a reading level recur, the narration flag’s behaviour within accounts and within sessions, duplicate and crash flags, changes across app versions, per-language feature coverage, and every nesting and crossing count the models require. It also reports missingness for every planned variable, overall and by language, age band, reading level, narration state and session order.

Three rules govern what the audit may change. A feature that is structurally unavailable in a language is not imputed: the language simply does not contribute it. A session missing an outcome is excluded from the model for that outcome alone, and a missing narration flag does not remove a session from a model that has no narration term. No variable is selected, transformed or discarded because of its association with reading time or with the rating.

The rating in Hypothesis 5 is optional and was absent for 25 of the 35 sessions in the structural sample, so high missingness is expected. Where the rating is missing for more than five per cent of otherwise eligible sessions, we will model availability from observed pre-response variables and report an inverse-probability-weighted analysis as a sensitivity check, with weight distributions and common support shown.

4.7 Analytic plan

We will fit all confirmatory models as Bayesian hierarchical models with the brms package and its Stan backend (Bürkner, 2017), standardising continuous predictors within language after transformation. Wherever the accompanying systematic review supports it, weakly informative priors are replaced by priors derived from that review, as described under Priors.

4.7.1 Outcome-blind dimension reduction

The observed text features are correlated by construction, because the same decision about what reading level a text is written for moves lexical, grammatical and discourse properties together. Principal component analysis is used to reduce them without treating them as effects of an unobserved latent cause (Crossley, 2025). It is conducted separately within the lexical, grammatical and discourse families. The affective measures are kept as separate secondary predictors, because valence and arousal are theoretically distinct and need not move together. Narrativity, though computed as a discourse feature, is likewise excluded from the component solution, because it enters every model as a separate secondary predictor and must not sit on both sides. Each distinct text contributes one row regardless of how often it was read, so a popular text does not determine the solution.

Counts are transformed with \(\log(1+x)\) and bounded proportions with an empirical-logit transform where these reduce skew. A feature is dropped from a language if more than 20 per cent of values are missing, if fewer than 80 per cent of texts receive a valid token-level match, or if its standard deviation after transformation is below 0.01. A language contributes to a family’s solution only if it has at least 100 distinct eligible texts and at least three valid features in that family, two of them anchors. Where two features correlate at \(|r| > .90\), the retained one is chosen in this order: lower missingness, then broader language coverage, then higher extraction reliability on a manually checked sample, then the measure listed first under Materials. No outcome correlation enters any of these decisions.

Features are standardised within language. To stop the largest language determining the solution, a correlation matrix is computed within each eligible language, the correlations are transformed to Fisher’s z, the matrices are averaged with equal weight per language, and the average is transformed back before the analysis (Revelle, 2023). Sampling adequacy requires individual Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin values above .50 and an overall value above .60. The number of components is set by parallel analysis against the 95th percentile of 10,000 random datasets (Horn, 1965). The scree plot is reported but does not override that rule. Where a family retains more than one component, promax rotation with \(\kappa\) = 4 is used, because linguistic properties may correlate (Biber, 1988). Regression scoring coefficients from the frozen, language-balanced solution are applied to each text’s within-language standardised features.

Each confirmatory component must load at least .30 in absolute value on at least two of its anchors. For LexicalDifficulty, the anchors are frequency, surprisal and word length. For GrammaticalLoad, they are dependency distance, subordination, clause density, sentence length and noun-phrase depth. For ReferentialContinuity, they are adjacent-sentence similarity, referential overlap and the density of causal and contrastive connectives. Age of acquisition contributes to the lexical family where a broad native norm set exists but is not an anchor, because the cross-linguistic set holds only 299 words per language. Connective density enters by relation type, and only the causal and contrastive types are anchors. The restriction is substantive: in 794 readers across 20 texts, causal and contrastive connectives raised comprehension, temporal ones did nothing and additive ones lowered it, so a single mixed count has no defined expected sign (Kleijn et al., 2019). Signs are fixed so that higher LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad mean a more demanding text and higher ReferentialContinuity a more explicitly connected one. If no component in a family satisfies the rule, the hypotheses resting on that family are reported as untestable. The complete pattern matrix, the component correlations, the variance explained, the language-specific matrices and the loading congruence for the five largest languages are reported. Language-specific and sparse solutions are sensitivity analyses, not alternative routes chosen after seeing an outcome.

As recorded under Data provenance, none of this has been run. The first fitting will follow in-principle acceptance.

4.7.2 Confirmatory models

In tier 1, we will estimate a model of within-language standardised log reading time per word with the three components, the age band, the narration flag, the interactions of each component with the age band and with narration, the reading level, log text length, narrativity, valence, arousal and the generated-or-classic indicator as fixed effects. Its grouping structure comprises varying intercepts for Text, Country and App version, varying intercepts and uncorrelated component slopes for Language, and a varying intercept for Account where the estimability rule above is met. Hypotheses 1 to 4 are evaluated in this model.

The Hypothesis 5 model has the same fixed and grouping structure and a cumulative-logit likelihood over the three rating categories, with category thresholds allowed to vary by language so that a difference in how families in different countries use the middle category is not read as a difference in text difficulty.

For tier 2, we will refit the same specification with a varying intercept for Account, decomposing accumulated reading into the account’s mean and the current session’s deviation from it, alongside session order and days since the previous session. The deviation term is the registered test for Hypothesis 6.

The tier-1 model also carries a distributional sub-model in which the residual standard deviation is itself predicted by the narration flag. The ratio of the two residual standard deviations is the compression diagnostic Hypothesis 4 depends on, and we will report it with that hypothesis.

Posterior predictive checks will assess calibration and residual structure by language, age band, reading level and narration state in every model.

4.7.3 Priors

For each confirmatory main effect, priors are derived from the accompanying systematic review through a deterministic pipeline whose rules were frozen before any extraction row existed, and which will be run, with its output archived, before any outcome datum is accessed. The review’s extracted estimates for the corresponding predictor family and outcome class are sign-harmonised so that facilitation always means faster reading, converted to a common standardised scale, and pooled by random-effects meta-analysis with one estimate per study per relation. The prior is Student’s \(t(3, \hat\mu, \sigma)\), where \(\hat\mu\) is the pooled estimate translated to the model’s scale and \(\sigma\) is twice the combined pooling and between-study uncertainty, with a floor of 0.10. The doubling and the floor guard against the gap between a laboratory comprehension test and a passively timed reading in a game.

Coefficients without adequate review evidence, which means every interaction term and any family supported by fewer than two studies, keep weakly informative priors: \(t(3, 0, 0.5)\) for standardised main effects and \(t(3, 0, 0.25)\) for interactions. Group-level and residual standard deviations take half-\(t(3, 0, 1)\) priors and any estimated correlation matrix takes LKJ(\(\eta\) = 2). Intercept priors are calibrated by prior-predictive simulation to the outcome scale, without reference to any predictor-outcome association. The derivation rules are summarised in Supplementary Section S6, and the full prior table with its forest plots will be archived before the extract is transferred and will accompany the Stage 2 report. We will refit every model with weakly informative priors throughout, and with the derived scales halved and doubled, and report conclusions under each.

4.7.4 Decision rules and diagnostics

For every confirmatory coefficient, we will report the posterior median, the 95 per cent highest-density interval, the probability of the predicted direction, the proportion of the posterior inside the region of practical equivalence, and a model-implied contrast on an interpretable scale. The region is \(|\beta| < 0.05\) standard deviations for a one-standard-deviation change in a predictor of reading time, and an odds ratio between 0.95 and 1.05 for the rating model. More than 95 per cent of the posterior inside the region is read as practical equivalence, less than 5 per cent inside it as a practically non-negligible association, and everything else as inconclusive.

We will run four chains of 4,000 iterations, half of them warm-up. Acceptable sampling requires \(\hat{R}\) below 1.01, bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 1,000 for every confirmatory parameter, no divergent transitions, satisfactory energy diagnostics and no unresolved maximum-treedepth warnings. Models failing these will be refitted with a higher target acceptance rate, greater tree depth and more iterations. If simplification is still needed, language-level slopes are removed in the fixed order lexical, then discourse, then grammatical, and every estimable varying intercept is retained. Every simplification and its diagnostic consequence will be reported. Variance inflation factors will be reported for the fixed-effect design matrix. A high value does not trigger an outcome-dependent deletion: the joint model remains primary, and single-family models are reported as a collinearity sensitivity analysis.

For Hypotheses 2, 3 and 4, we will compare the model with one omitting the relevant interaction, using Pareto-smoothed importance-sampling leave-one-out cross-validation, and report the expected log pointwise predictive density difference, its standard error and the Pareto-\(k\) diagnostics, with exact refits where importance sampling is unreliable. Predictive comparison supplements the coefficient-level summaries and does not gate them.

4.7.5 Outcome-neutral quality checks

Six checks run before the confirmatory models are interpreted, each with a stated pass criterion and a stated consequence of failure. The first four and the sixth are decided without reference to any confirmatory coefficient. The fifth is itself a registered clause of Hypothesis 5 and is evaluated with it.

The first is a permutation null. We will reassign texts at random to readings within language and reading level, and refit the tier-1 model 200 times. Every text coefficient must be practically equivalent to zero in at least 95 per cent of those refits. A coefficient that survives permutation means the pipeline is carrying structure that does not come from the pairing of a text with a reading, and we will withhold the confirmatory results until the leak is found and reported.

The second is a positive control on the timing column. Total elapsed time must rise with the story’s word count, with more than 99 per cent of the posterior above zero. This is close to a tautology if the column means what the dictionary says it means, which is why its failure would be informative: a timing field dominated by idle or backgrounded time need not track how much text there was to read.

The third asks whether the association is an artefact of platform assignment. We will re-estimate the three text coefficients within each reading level separately, and each must retain at least half the magnitude it had in the pooled model. The account on which text difficulty is merely assigned to children who differ in capacity predicts that these coefficients shrink towards zero when the level is held fixed. The account this protocol advances predicts that they do not.

The fourth is a set of three independence claims the theory object entails: conditional on the reading level and on whether a text was generated or classic, the three components should be close to independent across texts, narration should be unrelated to accumulated reading, and the difficulty rating should carry no further information about reading level once reading time is conditioned on. We will report each as an estimate with an interval. A clear failure does not invalidate the confirmatory tests, but it identifies a path the causal diagram omits, and it is reported as such.

The fifth is convergent validity, the ordering clause of Hypothesis 5, specified there and evaluated with it.

The sixth is a validation of the feature pipeline itself. We will check a stratified random sample of 50 texts per language by hand for tokenisation, sentence splitting and dependency attachment, and report the agreement statistics per language before any feature is joined to an outcome. A language whose labelled-attachment agreement with the hand-checked parse falls below .85 contributes to descriptive reporting but not to the grammatical component.

4.7.6 Design and precision analysis

Because we will analyse every eligible record, the question is not how large a sample to collect but what the available records can settle. Here that turns on a single quantity: the text coefficients are identified between distinct texts, and how many distinct texts the extract holds is unresolved.

We therefore simulated the planned crossed structure with pilotr, which reads the design from a portable specification file deposited alongside this protocol, sweeping the number of distinct texts at constant readings per text and separately sweeping readings per text at a constant number of texts (Bernabeu, 2026b). Simulation was on the within-language standardised scale, with the text intercept contributing 9 per cent of the variance, the child intercept 20 per cent and the residual the remainder, so a simulated coefficient is directly comparable with the region of practical equivalence. The simulation generated child variance and then handed the analysis a dataset in which each account contributes one reading, so that variance reached the fitted model as inflated residual. That is the less favourable of the two cases the estimability rule allows, so the requirements below are an upper bound on what the design needs. A coefficient the size of the region can never be declared determinately outside it, so we ran the sweep at twice that size, the smallest effect this protocol treats as worth reporting. A null condition with every coefficient set to zero established how reliably the design can declare practical equivalence, so an inconclusive verdict is predictable in advance.

For the three text coefficients, an effect of 0.10 standard deviations reached a determinate verdict nine times in ten at 738 distinct texts for lexical difficulty (95 per cent interval 640 to 843), 660 for grammatical load (593 to 731) and 634 for referential continuity (568 to 704). The interactions with the age band needed roughly twice as many: 1,230 texts for grammatical load (1,107 to 1,359) and 1,431 for referential continuity (1,276 to 1,596). The narration contrast needed 1,234 texts (1,113 to 1,361) before the design effect for imbalance is applied. Declaring practical equivalence when there is nothing there cost about the same: at 800 texts a main effect was correctly declared equivalent in 87 to 90 per cent of replicates and at 1,600 in every one, while the interactions reached 89 to 92 per cent only at 1,600. An inconclusive verdict is therefore predictable from the number of texts alone. The within-child term needed 563 children in tier 2 (463 to 674) contributing five readings each.

The second sweep is the more instructive. Holding the number of distinct texts at 250 and raising readings per text from 2 to 60 narrowed the interval on a main effect from 0.19 to 0.08 standard deviations, yet never carried it to a determinate verdict, reaching 0.70 at best. Over the same range the age-band interactions climbed from under 0.11 to between 0.87 and 0.90. Sessions and texts are therefore not interchangeable: a text coefficient is bounded by how many different texts the platform generated, whereas an interaction between a text property and a reader attribute gains from many different children meeting the same text. This matters because sessions are the quantity this platform has in abundance and distinct texts are the quantity in doubt, so a very large export could still leave Hypothesis 1 underpowered while answering Hypotheses 2 and 3.

Figure 3 shows both sweeps. The threshold each hypothesis has to clear is recorded in Table 1 and was fixed before the extract was released. A hypothesis whose required contrast is absent, or whose simulated interval cannot separate the region’s boundary from the effect, will be labelled untestable, or expected to be inconclusive, before its model is fitted.

Narration requires one further step. The simulation balances the narration contrast, whereas narration was on in 32 of the 35 sessions of the structural sample. An unbalanced binary contrast at prevalence \(p\) inflates the variance of its coefficient by \(1/(4p(1-p))\) relative to balance, so we will multiply the simulated requirement by that factor at the prevalence the provider-computed counts report, and state the adjusted requirement before fitting Hypothesis 4. At a prevalence of 0.09 the factor is about 3.0. This re-run, like the one promised for the rating availability in Table 1, replaces a single input of the archived scripts with the observed value and leaves every other line of the design analysis as deposited.

4.7.7 Sensitivity analyses

The following sensitivity analyses are pre-specified. We will refit the timing models with no plausibility bounds at all, with winsorisation at the 1st and 99th and at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles within language, and with posterior-predictive residual trimming. Where the censoring rule is triggered, we will report the censored and uncensored fits side by side. We will vary the tier-2 threshold from three distinct texts to two and to five, and add repeated readings with a repetition indicator. We will restrict analyses to the five largest languages, and fit language-specific models and component solutions with loading congruence reported. We will replace the component scores with sparse-component scores and with pre-specified equal-weight composites of the anchor features. For narration, we will restrict the comparison to accounts observed in both states, and adjust narrated timing for audio duration if that duration is ever supplied. We will compare the rating model under adjacent-category and cumulative-logit links, stratify it by language, and compare the inverse-probability analysis for rating non-response with the complete-case estimates. We will compare Text grouping with grouping on the attribute combination that generated the text. Finally, we will halve and double the prior scales and apply the alternative regions of practical equivalence, 0.03 and 0.10 standard deviations for reading time and odds-ratio bounds of 0.97 to 1.03 and 0.90 to 1.10 for the rating.

To ease comparison with the prior literature, we will report maximum-likelihood mixed models with the same fixed and estimable grouping structure in a supplementary table. They are descriptive, carry no confirmatory weight, and will not be used to resolve a Bayesian result.

4.8 What is not yet settled, and what follows from each answer

We put thirteen questions about the extract to the data controller on 7 August 2026, and they remain open at submission. Leaving them open is not a gap in the protocol so long as each has a decision rule attached in advance, and Table 2 attaches one to each. Every rule below is a function of the answer alone.

Five of these questions correspond to auxiliary assumptions recorded in the theory object, which is how the protocol keeps the statistical contingency and the theoretical commitment in the same place. If the answer to a question falsifies its assumption, the predictions that assumption protects are the ones the table reassigns.

4.9 Contingent hypotheses

We specify four further hypotheses in full here, outside the confirmatory set, because each depends on a fact about the extract that the data controller has not yet confirmed. Each carries an activation rule, and the rule is a function of that fact alone.

A contingent hypothesis activates only if its condition is confirmed in writing by the data controller before this protocol is submitted, and the confirmation is reproduced in the supplementary material. A condition confirmed after submission does not activate anything: the hypothesis is then reported at Stage 2 as exploratory, under that label, and is not counted among the confirmatory tests. This asymmetry is deliberate, because a hypothesis activated after review has not been reviewed.

4.9.1 C1: coherence and inferential comprehension

Condition: item-level comprehension responses exist and carry a recoverable question type. We would predict that, in an item-level model of response correctness, ReferentialContinuity × QuestionType is positive with inference questions coded 1 and literal questions 0, so that surface continuity helps more where the question requires integration (Aparicio et al., 2022; Freed & Cain, 2021; Pickren et al., 2022).

4.9.2 C2: narration and comprehension

Condition: item-level comprehension responses exist, and accounts are observed both with and without narration. We would predict that, within an account, narration is positively associated with response correctness after text, age band, reading level, accumulated reading and language are adjusted for. The expected size is small (Clinton-Lisell, 2023; Wood et al., 2018), and the coefficient identifies an association, not an effect.

4.9.3 C3: reading pace and comprehension

Condition: as C2, restricted to sessions without narration. We would predict that an account’s mean log reading time per word is negatively associated with response correctness, so that accounts with a faster habitual pace answer more accurately (Sortkær et al., 2021). The simultaneous within-account deviation term keeps this between-account association distinct from a difficult or interrupted session.

4.9.4 C4: machine-generated against human-authored text

Condition: the classic stories are confirmed to be fixed, human-authored texts, identical for all users within a language. We would predict that the three text components predict reading pace with coefficients of the same sign and of comparable magnitude in generated and in classic texts, and that the interaction of each component with the generated-or-classic indicator is practically equivalent to zero. This is a test of exchangeability, not of quality. A non-negligible interaction would mean that the properties governing how children read human-written stories govern their reading of generated ones differently, which matters well beyond this platform.

4.10 Randomisation, blinding and analytic independence

Nothing in this study is randomised. It is a secondary analysis of records generated by families using a commercial application, and no allocation, assignment or condition is under anyone’s control. The only random assignment anywhere in the protocol is the permutation quality check, where we reassign texts to readings at random precisely to destroy the association under test.

Blinding is structural: we cannot see the outcome data because the data controller holds them, under the sequestration arrangement described above. We will write, run and debug the outcome-model code against data simulated from the design specification deposited with this protocol, so that every script is complete and exercised before the real file exists on any machine of ours.

5 Conclusion

The trade this design makes is now explicit. It gives up experimental control, random assignment and any claim to a representative population, and it accepts an outcome measured once per story instead of once per fixation. In return it obtains hundreds to thousands of distinct texts as a random factor, ten or more languages, a number of readers per language beyond what a laboratory consortium can fund, and reading behaviour recorded where reading actually happens. We judge that trade worth making for this question in particular, because the question is whether text effects established under laboratory control survive outside it, and no better-controlled study can answer a question about what happens when control is absent.

The case does not rest on scale alone. Precision is bounded by the number of distinct texts, the one quantity the platform may lack, so a very large export could still leave the primary hypothesis underpowered. Every threshold in Table 1 was therefore fixed before the extract was released, and a hypothesis below its threshold will be declared underpowered before its model is fitted.

Each outcome is informative. If the three text coefficients are practically non-negligible, then the lexical, grammatical and discourse determinants of reading generalise from controlled settings to unsupervised, multilingual, machine-generated reading, which has not previously been shown. If they are practically equivalent to zero, that is a substantive negative result about the ecological reach of a large literature, obtained with precision the literature has not previously had. If the extract holds too few distinct texts, the protocol will say so before fitting and report that this platform cannot answer the question, which is itself worth knowing about a class of data source that others will be tempted to use.

The developmental hypotheses offer the clearest gain. No existing test of either interaction had more than 81 children, and the two accounts we register make opposite predictions on the scale this study uses. A determinate result in either direction adjudicates between them, and a posterior inside the region of practical equivalence would be evidence of absence rather than absence of evidence, which is a verdict no existing study has been large enough to deliver.

The limits should be equally clear. This study will not identify a causal effect of any text property, age band or narration setting. It will not estimate how fast children in general read, nor generalise beyond families who chose to install and use this application. It will say nothing about comprehension unless the contingent hypotheses activate on evidence that the necessary items exist. Where the data controller’s answers falsify an auxiliary assumption, the affected predictions are reassigned by the rules in Table 2, never reinterpreted after the fact.

More of children’s reading is migrating to platforms that record it, and the question of whether such records can support inference will be asked repeatedly and answered badly if it is answered informally. This protocol is one worked attempt at answering it well: sequester the data until the analysis is reviewed, register every decision rule as a function of counts, not of coefficients, calculate in advance what the design can and cannot settle, and name the accounts that a determinate result would refute.
