Text properties and children’s reading pace in a multilingual storytelling app

Abstract

Children increasingly read machine-generated stories on screens, and whether the text properties governing laboratory reading also govern it there is unknown. We will analyse logs from Let’s Story, in the Applaydu app, where children aged 7 and over read in any of 19 languages. Reading time per word will be modelled from word difficulty, grammatical load and how explicitly ideas are linked. Six confirmatory hypotheses concern these associations, their change with age, their selective weakening under narration, children’s difficulty ratings and accumulated in-app reading. Each association, estimated with readings grouped by text, child and language, is judged against a pre-set smallest effect of interest. Associations clearly beyond it would support the hypotheses, in either direction for the two that separate rival developmental accounts, and those clearly below it would show that laboratory effects do not transfer. Because families choose narration, texts and continued use, every estimate is a conditional association.

Introduction

A generation of children now does part of its reading on a screen, and some of what appears there was written by a machine. In the United Kingdom, one in five 8- to 18-year-olds read fiction on screen at least monthly in 2025, and the share reading print fiction had fallen from three in five to one in two since 2017 (Picton & Clark, 2026). The same report found that children reading fiction only on screen were disproportionately those who enjoyed reading least. Commercial storytelling applications generate a narrative to order, read it aloud in the family’s language and record what the child did with it. One such feature reported 2.4 million story sessions in its first six months (Ratner et al., 2025). Yet many parents interviewed in one small study wanted the plot and dialogue of a child’s story to remain human-authored, accepting machine-made illustrations only on that condition (Jin & Yuan, 2025). The behavioural record such platforms accumulate far exceeds any laboratory corpus of children’s reading, spans many languages and arises under the conditions that laboratory work excludes. We ask whether the text properties that govern reading under controlled conditions still govern it outside the laboratory, and whether their hold on the reader changes over the years in which decoding becomes automatic. Either answer bears on how far three determinants of reading established under experimental control extend to unsupervised reading, and on how their influence changes with age.

The laboratory findings are well established at three levels of linguistic description. In the Reading Systems Framework (Perfetti & Stafura, 2014), a word passes from identification through the lexicon to comprehension processes that include a parser, a text representation and a situation model. Within those processes, each word is integrated with the text read so far. This study’s three text components, LexicalDifficulty, GrammaticalLoad and ReferentialContinuity, relate in turn to identifying words, parsing sentences and integrating each sentence with those before it. None of them measures the situation model a reader builds.

At the level of the word, high-frequency words are recognised faster and fixated for less time than low-frequency words, and frequency and word-length effects on text reading are closely similar across 12 alphabetic languages (Kuperman et al., 2024). Those languages, including Greek and unpointed Hebrew, spell a word as a linear string of letters. Kuperman et al. (2024) left out Korean, which stacks its letters into syllable blocks, because the distribution of its word lengths differed too much from the others’. Children’s eye movements are driven by the frequency of the words they meet (H. S. S. L. Joseph et al., 2013). Compared with adult controls, children showed larger length and frequency effects (Tiffin-Richards & Schroeder, 2015), as predicted by an account on which the frequency effect tracks accumulated exposure (Brysbaert et al., 2018). The same developmental pattern appears outside alphabetic scripts (Lian et al., 2026), and the Lexical Quality Hypothesis describes it (Perfetti, 2007; Perfetti & Stafura, 2014). A word is retrieved quickly and reliably in proportion to how well specified and tightly bound its orthographic, phonological and semantic constituents are, and that quality is built by reading itself (Castles et al., 2018; Nation, 2017). On that account, a text’s lexical properties, a child’s age band (7 to 8, or 9 and over) and accumulated reading all bear on the cost of identifying a word. Hypothesis 1 tests the account through text properties, and Hypothesis 6 through accumulated in-app reading.

A second source of word-level cost is contextual. On surprisal theory, a word costs time in proportion to how unexpected it is given what precedes it (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008). The relation is close to linear in surprisal (Shain et al., 2024; Smith & Levy, 2013) and holds in 11 languages from five families (Wilcox et al., 2023). That linearity makes a text’s mean per-word surprisal the natural text-level summary of the cost, and the design adopts it, allowing for two qualifications. A text’s overall difficulty moderates how strongly per-word surprisal bears on reading time, so the text-level mean is not a neutral summary of a constant word-level effect (Chen et al., 2026). Frequency and surprisal are hard to separate, since dissociable frequency effects shrink as surprisal predictors improve (Oh & Schuler, 2025), which qualifies their reported additivity in naturalistic reading (Shain, 2024). LexicalDifficulty pools the two, so the confirmatory claim concerns their joint contribution and leaves the share of each unresolved. How subword tokens are aggregated to words affects psycholinguistic estimates (Giulianelli et al., 2024), and the unit over which surprisal should be defined is being reconsidered (Kiegeland et al., 2026), so the aggregation rule is fixed under Materials. In children, contextual facilitation appears in the later eye-movement measures that a whole-text duration absorbs (Tiffin-Richards & Schroeder, 2020).

Further lexical properties enter the design, each for a different reason. Concrete words are fixated for less time than abstract ones in natural reading, on every fixation measure where length and frequency were matched (Yao et al., 2024) and on first-fixation duration where they were covaried instead (Magnabosco & Hauk, 2024). That advantage is usually traced to perceptual grounding, on accounts that treat conceptual processing as partly modal and partly amodal (Kaup et al., 2025). A word’s age of acquisition bears on how quickly it is identified over and above its frequency (Juhasz, 2005). Function words are skipped far more often than content words (Rayner, 1998), so a text of higher lexical density (the proportion of content-word tokens) presents more tokens to be fixated and identified. Reading times for a word fall over repeated encounters, in children of 10 and 11 reading sentences (H. Joseph & Nation, 2018), so a lexical diversity index (McCarthy & Jarvis, 2010) tracks the share of tokens a reader meets for the first time. A word-level measure of informational cost predicts eye movements in first- and second-language readers of diverse writing systems (Kimchi et al., 2025), though its authors test no text-level aggregate of the kind used here.

At the level of the sentence, structure imposes a further cost. Mean dependency distance indexes the distance over which a dependency must be resolved, and clause density, subordination and noun-phrase depth index how many dependencies are open at once. Structural cost is not reducible to predictability. In 368 adults reading syntactically challenging sentences, language-model surprisal from 409 estimators approximated the early effects of disambiguation but not the additional rereading that repairing a structure incurred (Timkey et al., 2026). For that reason, the grammatical features form a distinct family and are never absorbed into surprisal. Gibson’s (1998) theory separates the cost of keeping a prediction alive from that of integrating the element that completes it. Retrieval-based accounts place the cost at the point of resolution, where intervening and similar material slows retrieval (Lewis & Vasishth, 2005). Expectation-based accounts add that the material before a head can make the head more predictable (Levy, 2008), so in head-final configurations a longer dependency need not cost more. A clause-final verb has been read faster after longer intervening material in German and Hindi (Konieczny, 2000; Vasishth & Lewis, 2006), and the strength of a locality effect is expected to vary with a language’s statistics (Futrell et al., 2020). The grammatical slope is therefore allowed to vary by language in every model below.

Hypothesis 2 asks whether children bear these costs disproportionately. A prior question is whether a grammatical cost appears at all when reading time is measured over whole texts, as here. In naturalistic corpus reading by adults, a distance-based integration cost predicted reading times at nouns and auxiliaries but not at words in general, whereas word predictability predicted them throughout (Demberg & Keller, 2008). In the Multilingual Eye-movement Corpus, global measures of syntactic and lexical complexity (among them the longest dependency path, the proportion of embedded sentences and mean sentence length) predicted adults’ passage reading negligibly once passage length was accounted for (Kuperman et al., 2025). At the sentence level, that study found effects of any size only for sentence length and position. Its lexical measures proved as negligible as its syntactic ones, but its authors separate such global measures from the aggregate properties of a passage’s individual words. LexicalDifficulty is anchored on those aggregates (mean frequency, word length and surprisal), whose word-level effects are well replicated across languages (Kuperman et al., 2024; Wilcox et al., 2023). The grammatical question remains open for children reading whole stories, and Hypothesis 1 is framed so that either answer is informative.

At the level of the passage, the property of interest is whether the text states the connection between one idea and the next or leaves the reader to supply it. Sentence-to-sentence semantic similarity, overlap in referring expressions and the density of explicit connectives together index how much of that work the text’s surface has already done (Crossley et al., 2019). These are measures of cohesion, a property of the text, whereas coherence is a property of the representation the reader builds, and cohesion eases its construction without guaranteeing it (McNamara et al., 2010). Referential overlap descends from the rule that connects propositions sharing an argument (Kintsch & Dijk, 1978), which the construction-integration model retains in the weights it assigns to propositions close together in the text base (Kintsch, 1988). Semantic similarity between adjoining sentences approximates that propositional analysis (Foltz et al., 1998). An explicit relational marker speeds processing of the segment it introduces without changing what is recalled (Sanders & Noordman, 2000). Multi-dimensional characterisations of difficulty built from such indices outperform classical readability formulae (Crossley et al., 2023). Evaluated against eye movements, though, readability scorers account for real-time reading ease only partially (Gruteke Klein et al., 2026). Partly for that reason, this study models the psycholinguistic features and uses no composite readability score.

The systematic review accompanying this protocol refines these laboratory findings in ways that shape the design, and Supplementary Section S2 tabulates the studies closest to this study in method and modality. Text difficulty does not reduce to word frequency. In German fourth graders, estimated lexical coverage predicted comprehension non-linearly (Ludewig et al., 2023). In 158 children aged 8 to 11, word frequency, pronoun density, concreteness and deep cohesion each explained unique variance in passage comprehension once decoding and oral language were controlled, and lexical arousal explained variance beyond them (Pickren et al., 2022). That result warrants including affective and discourse variables alongside conventional readability measures. Syntactic complexity impaired comprehension accuracy and slowed recall in bilingual children (Wasito Kirana et al., 2025). In 5,133 second graders reading informational text, word concreteness, referential cohesion and deep cohesion each predicted oral reading rate beyond classical readability formulae (Tortorelli, 2020). In 78 first graders, the mean log frequency of a text’s words predicted reading accuracy, words correct per minute and maze comprehension (Bruner et al., 2026). Both samples read aloud and fall within or below this study’s younger band. They establish the association with oral reading rate, without fixing its size among children of 9 and over, or in the app, where a child may read silently, aloud or along with narration.

Two further strands concern the reader and the reading context. On the reader’s side, longitudinal work shows that a child’s reading history is not a simple quantity of practice. In random-intercept cross-lagged models from ages 7 to 16, weaker early reading predicted less subsequent leisure reading, while later within-person increases in book reading predicted gains in comprehension (Torppa et al., 2020). A 10-year study likewise found reciprocal associations between skill and print exposure, with the skill-to-practice path most visible early and accumulated exposure more predictive later (van Bergen et al., 2021). Accumulated use of an app reflects both practice and the stable differences in skill and motivation that produced the use, so Hypothesis 6 separates within-child change from between-child differences. The setting matters as well as the quantity. Digital leisure reading and print reading do not relate to comprehension in the same way (Altamura et al., 2025), and digital leisure reading is associated with comprehension independently of print (Altamura et al., 2026). Hypothesis 6 counts only reading inside one app.

As for the reading context, read-aloud narration is often treated as a uniform benefit, but the evidence does not support that view. A meta-analysis of 30 reading-while-listening studies, 62 effects over a total N of 1,945, found a small pooled comprehension advantage (g = 0.18). There was no reliable advantage where ordinary reading was self-paced (g = 0.06, 95 per cent CI [-0.07, 0.19]), and a larger one only where the experimenter paced it (g = 0.41, 95 per cent CI [0.13, 0.70]) (Clinton-Lisell, 2023). Among students with reading difficulties, text-to-speech tools showed a moderate pooled effect (d = 0.35) that fell to d = 0.15, with an interval including zero, in within-participant designs (Wood et al., 2018). Neither synthesis identifies the mechanism, which a large naturalistic corpus is well placed to address. A spoken rendition may work by supplying word identity, in which case it should remove much of the cost of lexical difficulty while leaving the cost of connecting ideas intact. Alternatively, the audio’s duration may set narrated reading time. Hypothesis 4 is designed to distinguish these decoding-scaffolding and audio-pacing accounts.

The generated stories studied here are a population of stimuli produced by one process, and a process need not distribute text properties as human authorship does. Comparisons in other domains report that large-language-model output has a distinct noun-heavy, informationally dense register even when prompted otherwise (Reinhart et al., 2025). Human writing in those comparisons shows more scattered sentence-length distributions and more varied vocabulary than model output (Muñoz-Ortiz et al., 2024). In creative writing, the domain closest to these stories, stylometric comparison separates the two on measurable features of style (O’Sullivan, 2025). Whether the lexical, grammatical and discourse determinants established on human-written text describe reading across such a population is a question about the scope of those findings (Haeffel & Cobb, 2022; Yarkoni, 2022).

To gauge how much published work combines this study’s topics, we searched Scopus on 26 August 2026 for records from 2018 onwards. The search returned 3,701 records on children’s reading comprehension, 6,592 on computational text features and 133,132 on platform log data. The pairwise intersections were 17, 28 and 45 records, and the three-way intersection was empty. The search covered one database under one set of expressions and missed the log analysis of this platform described below, although it had appeared two months earlier. The empty cell shows only that the intersection is thin and unevenly indexed, not that it is unoccupied. Figure 1 shows the sets, and Supplementary Section S4 records the search expressions and the publication trend. The search script, which archives the counts, is deposited with this protocol (Bernabeu, 2026c).

Over the same window, records combining children’s reading with machine-generated text went from none before 2024 to two in 2024, six in 2025 and four in the first eight months of 2026. The studies are classroom experiments that report gains in comprehension and motivation but describe the texts only qualitatively (Balantekin, 2026; Bulut & Aktepe, 2026). Generated and human-written texts have been compared directly, though not yet in children reading for themselves. Generated stories had fewer personal pronouns and more positive emotion than 100 human-written ones, and adult readers were less transported by them (Appel et al., 2025). The models tested for children’s stories could not hold their vocabulary to a level appropriate for younger readers (M. Valentini et al., 2023), a limitation that bears directly on the lexical component.

Let’s Story, a feature of the Applaydu app developed by Gameloft and Ferrero International, combines the three topics of the Scopus search. Children choose a hero, a companion, a setting, a plot and a theme, as the session table records (Supplementary Section S8), and a generative model writes a narrative around those choices (Ratner et al., 2025). The app presents the narrative as an illustrated storybook, reads it aloud at the family’s option (Ratner et al., 2025) and logs every completed reading. Comparable platforms have been studied one feature at a time. Booton et al. (2023) reviewed 11 studies and found inbuilt narration to aid comprehension and word learning. Log data from a research-oriented platform track ability, with reading speed in the BookBites app correlating with standardised scores at r = .39 to .48 (Sortkær et al., 2021). The commercial edutainment sector, with a far larger audience, is described in the literature we identified mainly by content analyses and small experiments (Chatzopoulos et al., 2023; Furlong et al., 2025).

One analysis of logs from Let’s Story itself has been published. Over 419,700 story sessions in 18 languages across the 30 days following the feature’s launch in November 2024, Ratner & Murphy (2026) describe how children aged 4 to 9 meet challenge. They report completion, the post-reading difficulty rating and where in a story abandoned sessions stopped. The analysis is descriptive by design and drawn from aggregated dashboard counts, so its authors state that it cannot follow an individual reader. Because it models no reading time and its summaries lacked the session-level structure a within-child term requires, it leaves every association registered here untested. Its window was the first month after launch, whereas ours opens more than a year later, so the novelty effects its authors mention apply much less here. The study does establish that the difficulty rating, the completion requirement and the session record can be analysed in large samples on this platform.

The theoretical commitments behind the hypotheses are set out in a machine-checkable theory object deposited with this protocol. It names twelve constructs, eighteen propositions, six auxiliary assumptions and six rival accounts. Each proposition states its mechanism, and each of the ten predictions is recorded with the proposition it derives from and, for seven, the rival it is diagnostic against. The object claims that the time a child spends per word is set by the costs a text imposes at the word, the sentence and the passage. Three reader capacities reduce those costs: the automaticity of print-to-sound mapping, verbal working memory and the readiness to supply an unstated connection. A spoken rendition bears more on the first than on the third. The platform records none of the three, and the age band stands in for them. Five further propositions extend the same constructs to the quality of the mental model a child ends with. They could be examined only in exploratory analyses at Stage 2, and only if comprehension items prove to exist, as set out under Contingent hypotheses. Figure 2, generated from the object, draws its constructs and the paths between them. The object satisfies all twelve items of the versioned rigour checklist in theoryforge, scoring 90.7 out of 100 (Supplementary Section S1) (Bernabeu, 2026d). Its causal graph is acyclic, so the independence claims it entails can be derived formally. Before interpreting the confirmatory models, we will test three of them, listed with their criterion under Outcome-neutral quality checks, and report any failure.

This study is observational throughout. Families choose the stories, the reading level and whether narration is on, and decide whether to keep using the app. A laboratory experiment establishes that a text property can change reading when an experimenter controls the text, the setting and the task. The further question, whether the property does so when nothing is controlled, concerns generalisation and cannot be answered in the setting that produced the original result (Nastase et al., 2020; Yarkoni, 2022). Whether a relation holds in a new population of texts, languages and readers therefore tests the account that predicts it (Haeffel & Cobb, 2022). In the logs of a vocabulary-learning app, a laboratory effect of valence held whereas one of concreteness did not (Wild & Kuperman, 2024), though those users were adults and the outcome was learning.

The platform offers three gains in return for that loss of control, beginning with stimulus sampling. Reading research has known since Clark (1973) that treating a handful of experimenter-written texts as a fixed set inflates confidence in conclusions meant to hold for texts in general. The mixed-model solution has been available for over a decade (Judd et al., 2012). The design treats Text as a crossed random factor over hundreds of texts that families selected, which is both the prescribed remedy and, in Brunswik’s sense, a representative design (Dhami et al., 2004). A varying intercept does not by itself make an inference general where the variation between texts is systematic (Bear & Phillips, 2022), so the model includes the systematic sources of variation the platform records. Because an average over texts can hide the variation between them (Simonsohn et al., 2025), the text-level deviations will be displayed against the component scores at Stage 2. We avoid the phrase ecological validity, used in at least two senses, Brunswik’s and Orne’s (Kihlstrom, 2021), and argued to be too ill-defined to bear on generalisation (Holleman et al., 2020). Our claim is the narrower, checkable one about how the stimuli were sampled.

The second gain, linguistic breadth, matters because English dominates cognitive science to a degree that warps which mechanisms are studied and licenses overgeneralisation from one atypical language to the species (Blasi et al., 2022). Reading is among the worst affected, because writing systems differ in ways that matter for every stage of processing. The most ambitious response to date, the Multilingual Eye-movement Corpus, assembled eye-tracking during text reading in 13 languages, since extended to 21 (Siegelman et al., 2022; Siegelman et al., 2025). Its authors report that the existing empirical base is insufficient for cross-linguistic comparison. A commercial platform covers many of the same languages incidentally, with far more readers per language, and its readers are children, whereas the corpus records skilled adults. The platform records only whole-story durations, so it gains breadth at the cost of the resolution an eye-movement record gives.

The lexical and continuity associations are expected in their predicted directions in every language the extract contains. The app offers 19: Arabic, Dutch, English, French, German, Greek, Hebrew, Indonesian, Italian, Korean, Polish, Portuguese, Romanian, Russian, Simplified Chinese, Spanish, Thai, Traditional Chinese and Turkish. The operations reading recruits, mapping a written form to sound and to meaning and relating it to what came before, are common to writing systems that differ in almost everything else (Frost, 2012; Verhoeven & Perfetti, 2022). The cross-linguistic evidence reviewed above bears this out for the lexical properties in alphabetic scripts and in Chinese, although it leaves out Korean and Thai. Averaged over languages, the grammatical association is expected in its predicted direction, but expectation-based accounts allow it to weaken or vanish in head-final structures, as in German and Dutch verb-final clauses and throughout Korean and Turkish. Because head-final languages also minimise dependency length much less than head-initial ones (Futrell et al., 2015), the grammatical association should vary most across languages. The lexical association should vary in magnitude with how a script encodes the word, most of all where it departs from a linear string of letters separated by spaces. Korean stacks its letters into syllable blocks, Chinese characters stand for syllables and morphemes, Chinese and Thai leave the boundaries between words unmarked, and Arabic and Hebrew usually leave short vowels unwritten. Orthographic depth may add further differences among the alphabetic languages, since children’s length and frequency effects are orthography-specific even where adults’ converge (Schroeder et al., 2022). No comparable reason has been derived for referential continuity, which operates above the word. These expectations concern the language-varying component slopes, reported for every language, though no verdict in Table 1 rests on any one of them.

The third gain is behaviour logged by the app as it occurred. Logged and self-reported digital media use diverge systematically enough to have warranted a meta-analysis of the discrepancy (Parry et al., 2021). Among 109 French primary-school children, parents’ estimates of weekly reading time were three times the logged figure, which predicted fluency better (Brossette et al., 2026).

Against these gains stands the strongest statistical objection to any large non-random dataset. Meng’s (2018) decomposition shows that the error of a sample mean is the product of three quantities: data quality, data quantity and the difficulty of the problem. Data quality is the correlation between the recorded value and whether it was recorded at all, and probability sampling keeps it near zero. Once it is no longer near zero, the population size stops cancelling, and estimation error grows with the square root of the population for any given sample size. Bradley and colleagues (2021) gave the canonical demonstration. Two surveys collecting about 250,000 responses a week and 75,000 a fortnight overestimated United States COVID-19 vaccine uptake by 17 and 14 percentage points, with margins of error so small that the wrong answers looked precise. A well-designed panel of about 1,000 responses a week, by contrast, was accurate. Every point of selection here is a candidate for that correlation: who owns a tablet, who installs an app tied to a confectionery brand, who registers consent for research use, who switches narration off and who finishes a story.

Meng’s result, however, concerns the estimation of a population mean, and this study estimates none. Its estimands are conditional associations inside the sample, within a language and within a reading level. A point of selection that depends on the reader alone, such as who owns a tablet or who registers consent, changes which children a slope describes without distorting it. Where the reader attribute also moderates the slope, the estimate becomes the average slope across the families who use the platform. An estimate of a heterogeneous association from a non-random sample should be interpreted as such an average (Bryan et al., 2021). We will report these coefficients as estimates for children who read on this platform, never as population values for children in general, and will report no population mean of reading speed for any group. A slope is distorted only when selection is a common effect of the text property, or of something correlated with it, and of the outcome or of an unmeasured cause of the outcome (Elwert & Winship, 2014; Hernán et al., 2004; Lamp & MacKinnon, 2025). That condition is narrower than the one that biases a mean. Of the points of selection listed above, only completion meets it. Narration is a state the models condition on, so the selection it involves affects the denominators of the Hypothesis 4 proportions and is addressed with that hypothesis.

The extract that the app’s data controller will transfer contains completed readings only, so the analysis conditions on completion, which is plausibly a common effect of the text’s difficulty and the reader’s capacity. Conditioning on a common effect can induce an association between its causes where none existed (Rohrer, 2018), and the direction here is predictable. If harder texts are abandoned more often, the hard texts that remain are disproportionately those read by children who could manage them, which attenuates the coefficients Hypothesis 1 predicts. If younger children abandon demanding texts more often, their remaining readings of such texts are the more selected and their slopes the more attenuated (Elwert & Winship, 2014; Hernán & Monge, 2023). On the sign convention given below, that attenuation pushes the grammatical interaction positive and the continuity interaction negative, towards the bottleneck-release and cue-utilisation accounts of Hypotheses 2 and 3 respectively. The bias therefore works against our predictions, so a non-negligible result withstands the objection better than a null does. Where the data controller can supply the completion rate by age band, we will report it with every verdict on Hypotheses 1 to 3. If the younger band’s rate is the lower, a verdict supporting the bottleneck-release or cue-utilisation account will be reported as also consistent with differential attrition.

A reader can cope with a demanding text without abandoning it, by reading it less carefully. When reading speed was manipulated, comprehension held across a broad range of rates and fell only at the fastest, around 405 words per minute, while the influence of word properties on eye movements persisted throughout (Schwalm et al., 2026). That result places the 400-words-per-minute plausibility bound close to the rate at which comprehension gave way, so the bound removes page-flipping but keeps fast, still comprehending reading. The same study gives no warrant for assuming that faster readings show weaker text effects, since its word-level effects did not weaken with speed. Neither the extract nor the design can separate a skimmed reading from a fluent one, so we register the ambiguity without assigning it a direction.

We do not use causal verbs for variables that families chose. The questions are nonetheless causal, and we say so because euphemism has been argued to damage observational research by obscuring what is being asked (Grosz et al., 2020; Hernán, 2018). We state the causal structure in the theory object’s causal graph, derive the adjustment set from it, register the rival explanations it implies and report associations conditional on that adjustment.

Secondary data invite the analytic flexibility that preregistration exists to remove, and the field has standards for preregistering analyses of existing data (Van den Akker et al., 2021; Weston et al., 2019). The arrangement described under Data provenance goes further, since the data controller holds the extract, unanalysed, and will transfer it only after in-principle acceptance. As a result, the most common objection to observational work, that the analyst saw the data before choosing the analysis, does not arise. Withholding the outcome until the analysis is fixed is the principle of blind analysis (Dutilh et al., 2021; MacCoun & Perlmutter, 2015).

The analysis estimates a few pre-specified associations and does not attempt to predict an individual child’s reading. We calculated the precision attainable for each without access to the extract and state in advance which of them the available records cannot resolve. Group-level associations need not describe any individual, since variance around an expected value was two to four times larger within people than within groups (Fisher et al., 2018). The within-child analysis uses tier 2, the user accounts with at least three eligible readings of distinct texts, to separate each child’s own change from differences between children. The two are never reported as a single quantity.

The design allows for three features of the platform and its records. First, the reading level a family sets determines which version of a text the platform presents, so text difficulty is partly assigned by a choice that also reflects the child’s capacity. We will estimate the text coefficients within reading level and check whether they persist under that restriction. Second, the outcome is a whole-story rate, and we state below what such a rate can and cannot index. Third, the data contain no measure of a child’s reading skill, vocabulary or language background. The reading language a family selects need not be the child’s first language, so every coefficient below averages over children reading in a first and in a second language. In adult bilinguals reading paragraphs, frequency effects were larger in the second language than in the first and grew as second-language exposure fell (Whitford & Titone, 2012). A reading language is not a language background, and the country-by-language analysis registered under Sensitivity analyses is the extract’s only means of addressing the difference. Without measures of skill and vocabulary, the age band stands in for a bundle of developing capacities.

Accounts of reading development suggest what the age band can index when no measure of skill is available. Under the simple view, reading comprehension is the product of decoding and language comprehension, and decoding’s share of the variance falls across the primary grades (Gough & Tunmer, 1986; Hoover & Gough, 1990; Lonigan et al., 2018). On automaticity theory, that share falls because identification that runs without deliberate attention releases capacity for integration (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974; Perfetti, 2007). Phonological recoding on each new word is the mechanism by which reading itself produces that automaticity (Share, 1995), which ties Hypothesis 6 to that theory. In a longitudinal eye-movement study, reading efficiency rose by 36 per cent between grades 2 and 4, and linguistic skill determined the change whereas oculomotor skill did not (Huestegge et al., 2009). Among 194 Greek-speaking children, silent reading speed rose from 111 words per minute in grade 3 to 166 in grade 6, through fewer and shorter fixations and longer saccades (Gleni et al., 2026). In the same study, those oculomotor parameters explained variance in speed beyond vocabulary and cognitive measures. The age band is therefore interpreted as a coarse, aggregate proxy for reading experience and the automaticity it confers, not for any single capacity.

The platform sets the boundary between the age bands. It describes its audience as children of 4 to 9 (Ratner & Murphy, 2026), so the older band is expected to lie mostly at its lower edge, and its upper edge is not recorded. Whether change across such a boundary is governed by attained reading level or maturing oculomotor control is still debated (Justino & Kolinsky, 2023; Louwen et al., 2026). These data can address that question only through the finer-age sensitivity analysis registered below.

Every hypothesis but Hypothesis 5 has the same outcome, the time a child spends per word over a whole story. In the laboratory, reading time is informative about processing because a word is fixated for about as long as it takes to process (Just & Carpenter, 1980). The properties that lengthen fixations predict passage-level eye-movement measures on texts of more than 200 words, a relation shown so far in second-language readers of English (Nahatame & Yamaguchi, 2025). The theory object records this inference as the auxiliary assumption that elapsed time indexes effort, on which six of its ten predictions depend. The positive control on recorded reading time, registered under Outcome-neutral quality checks, puts that assumption at risk. A whole-text rate is more than the sum of its word-level costs, since the efficiency of processing items in a series explained variance in reading connected text once discrete word reading was controlled (Romero et al., 2024). The components are properties of the text, so differences in readers’ efficiency are absorbed by the age band, the residual and, where it is fitted, the Account intercept. We describe the outcome as reading pace, an index of the processing demand a text imposes, and not as a measure of comprehension or fluency, because nothing in these data supports either description.

The theory object registers one rival interpretation of the outcome, the engagement account. It traces time on a story to how engaging the story is, whereas this study traces it to how demanding the text is. Among 122 sixth graders reading silently, passage difficulty, rated situational interest and their interaction all predicted time on task, and interest accounted for the most variance (Welland & Burns, 2025). Two registered predictions are marked diagnostic against the engagement account. For the continuity coefficient, that account would produce a non-negligible positive value, since a text that states more of its connections might hold a child longer. The theory predicts instead that such a text should let the child finish sooner. Hypothesis 5 is also diagnostic, because a difficulty rating formed from effort the child noticed expending should rank the three components as reading time does, but a rating tracking enjoyment need not. Neither prediction alone rules out the engagement account.

Two of the six confirmatory hypotheses below concern interactions between a text property and the age band. Both are registered as two-sided tests that adjudicate between rival accounts, because the direct developmental evidence in this age range supports neither sign.

The age band and narration enter the models as deviations from their observed prevalence in tier 1 (the first eligible reading of each text by each user account). A reading scores 1 for the older band, or for narration on, and 0 otherwise, minus the proportion of tier-1 readings in that state. Each component’s main effect is then its prevalence-weighted average slope across the two states, free of design effects from imbalance. Each interaction is the difference in slope between the two states, positive when the slope is larger in the older band or under narration. The reading-time outcome is log-transformed and standardised within language, so a coefficient is a within-language standardised slope. The region of practical equivalence, the range of effects treated as negligible, has a half-width of 0.05 standard deviations for such a slope. Hypothesis 4 concerns a difference between two attenuation proportions, with a region of half-width 0.05 on that difference. Hypothesis 5 uses the child’s own three-point difficulty rating, and its region has a half-width of log(1.05) on the log-odds scale, corresponding to odds ratios between 0.952 and 1.05.

LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad are oriented so that higher scores mean a more demanding text, and ReferentialContinuity so that higher scores mean a text that states more of the links between its ideas. The orientations are fixed before any component score is linked to outcome data. Because the main effects of grammatical load and referential continuity run in opposite directions, the same developmental claim takes opposite signs in the two interactions. Grammatical load is associated with slower reading, so a weaker association in older children makes a positive slope smaller and the interaction negative. Referential continuity is associated with faster reading, so a weaker association in older children makes a negative slope less negative and the interaction positive.

Children in the two age bands differ in overall reading rate, and a purely multiplicative difference in rate produces a spurious interaction on a raw time scale (Faust et al., 1999). On the log scale, such a difference appears only as a main effect, so every interaction reported here is a difference in proportional cost.

The log transformation leaves two other sources of interaction in place. First, an interaction can arise from an additive component of the interval. If the time a word costs is a base decoding time plus a grammatical increment, the log-scale grammatical slope is that increment divided by the base. A reader with a smaller base then shows a larger proportional slope even when the increment is identical. The bottleneck-release account posits an additive decoding component of this kind, so a positive Hypothesis 2 interaction is that account’s expected signature on this scale, not an artefact of the transformation. Second, part of any whole-story interval is spent on the illustrations, on turning pages and away from the task. Children’s attention to the text of an illustrated book rises across the primary grades (Roy-Charland et al., 2007), so the non-reading share may be larger in the younger band. The younger band’s proportional slopes would then be smaller, moving the Hypothesis 2 interaction towards the positive sign and the Hypothesis 3 interaction towards the negative one. No registered test decides whether a smaller base interval reflects faster decoding or less time away from the text, although the lexical interaction below and the page-count refit under Sensitivity analyses bear on it. A practically non-negligible interaction of either sign will be reported with that limitation.

The remaining age-band interaction, LexicalDifficulty × AgeBand, has no registered test, and the theory object’s propositions assign it no sign. The bottleneck-release account, which the theory object registers as a rival, nonetheless predicts a negative or null interaction, for reasons set out with Hypothesis 3. The eye-movement literature establishes larger absolute lexical effects in children (Tiffin-Richards & Schroeder, 2015) without showing whether they are larger as a proportion of a slower baseline, the quantity a log outcome measures. The developmental evidence comes from raw latencies on isolated words. Across grades 2 to 4, word length and regularity effects declined while frequency effects persisted (Heß et al., 2026). In Italian children of grades 1 to 3, sensitivity to word length fell with age, in a study that manipulated length alone (Zoccolotti et al., 2005).

Expressing the outcome per word addresses one confound and leaves another. In the standard cohesion manipulation, the more cohesive version of a text is 20 to 40 per cent longer. A whole-text duration would then show higher cohesion slowing reading for arithmetic reasons alone. Dividing by word count and retaining log text length as a covariate closes that path, and a raw duration is never used as an outcome. A per-word rate still includes the extra time readers spend at sentence boundaries, time whose cause is uncertain (Rayner et al., 2000; Warren et al., 2009), and a text of shorter sentences has more boundaries per word. The boundary time works against the expected sign of sentence length taken alone.

Hypothesis 1: text properties and reading pace. We predict that, across distinct texts and within reading level and language, LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad will each be positively associated with log reading time per word, and ReferentialContinuity negatively. The three coefficients are evaluated and labelled separately under the interpretations in Table 1, and the failure of one does not suppress reporting of the others. A positive continuity coefficient is not labelled a reverse cohesion effect, because that effect is a learning outcome (McNamara et al., 1996; McNamara & Kintsch, 1996; O’Reilly & McNamara, 2007). On the construction-integration model, the effect arises when a less cohesive text makes the knowledgeable reader infer more. The extra inference would appear on a time scale as slower reading of that text, in the predicted direction. A grammatical coefficient practically equivalent to zero would extend to children the negligible passage-level effect of global complexity measures reported for adults (Kuperman et al., 2025). That interpretation does not apply to the lexical component, whose anchors are word-level aggregates the adult study did not test at passage level.

The narration rule follows from how narration is distributed. Narration was on in about nine sessions in ten of the structural sample, so each prevalence-weighted main effect is dominated by the narrated slope. If the audio sets the pace of narrated sessions, the narrated continuity slope converges on zero and the lexical slope on the slope of audio duration per word, as explained under Hypothesis 4. The compression diagnostic, the ratio of the narrated to the unnarrated residual standard deviation, signals such pacing when it falls below the cut-off registered under Confirmatory models. Where the diagnostic indicates compression and the distinct texts with unnarrated readings meet a component’s threshold, that component’s Hypothesis 1 verdict rests on the derived unnarrated slope, with the prevalence-weighted slope also reported. Each component’s threshold is set by the seventh adjustment registered under Design and precision analysis. If compression is indicated but the texts with unnarrated readings fall short of that threshold, the prevalence-weighted verdict is retained and reported alongside the unnarrated slope. In that case, an inside-region continuity verdict or a non-negligible lexical verdict is reported as consistent with the audio having set the pace. The rule does not depend on the sign of any coefficient.

Hypothesis 2: grammatical load and the age band. We register the GrammaticalLoad × AgeBand interaction as a two-sided adjudicating test, because developmental accounts predict opposite signs on this study’s scale. On the capacity account, formulated for individual differences among adults, a grammatical dependency costs time in proportion to how close its maintenance comes to exhausting the working memory available (Just & Carpenter, 1992; King & Just, 1991). Our developmental extension predicts a negative interaction, because working-memory capacity grows steadily across the primary-school years (Gathercole et al., 2004) and the same structure should then cost the older reader proportionally less. Consistent with that extension, span measures in a cross-sectional sample of French-speaking children of 5 to 12 predicted complex-syntax comprehension, repetition and production over and above age (Delage & Frauenfelder, 2019). The experience account predicts the same sign but attributes the change with age to growing exposure to complex structure, since exposure makes such structure cheaper (MacDonald & Christiansen, 2002). Evidence that children with more print exposure produce relative clauses more as adults do (Montag & MacDonald, 2015) concerns production, not the cost of reading such a structure.

The bottleneck-release account predicts the opposite sign, locating the change with age in the base over which the proportion is taken and in how fully structure is built on that base. As print-to-sound mapping automatises, the base decoding time shrinks (LaBerge & Samuels, 1974), leaving the reader with representations whose quality makes retrieval cheap (Perfetti, 2007). An unchanged absolute grammatical cost then occupies a larger proportion of a smaller base, and the interaction is positive. As for how fully structure is built, comprehenders often leave syntactic structure shallow or unresolved (Ferreira et al., 2002), although the evidence for such shallow processing is mixed (Frances, 2024). Globally ambiguous relative-clause attachments are read faster than disambiguated ones when readers expect only superficial questions (Swets et al., 2008), an advantage that may owe more to reanalysing the disambiguated versions than to leaving structure unresolved (Staub, 2026). Reading in the app resembles that low-demand condition, since the app, as far as the data controller has disclosed, asks children nothing about a story’s content, which question (m) in Table 2 asks it to confirm. Children of 8 to 10 show the same signatures of shallow processing as adults, and the older among them attend more to the punctuation that marks clause boundaries (Wonnacott et al., 2016). On this view, a younger reader who builds shallower structure incurs less of a dependency’s cost. In its developmental form, the capacity account is the theory object’s own proposition, and the bottleneck-release account is registered there as its rival. A positive interaction would therefore count both against that proposition and for the rival.

The premise of our developmental extension, that working memory limits the processing of complex syntax, is less secure in this age range than the adult evidence suggests. Across 197 studies, the relation between working memory and reading was moderate, r = .29, and its contribution to comprehension was accounted for by decoding and vocabulary where those were controlled (Peng et al., 2018). For relative-clause comprehension, the link was narrow in both studies we identified. In German-speaking 7-year-olds, the relation was to digit span and held for object relatives alone (Arosio et al., 2012), and in French- and German-speaking children, verbal working memory correlated with object-relative comprehension only in French (Solaimani et al., 2025). The only study we found that related span to online processing in children ran against the capacity account. Among 250 children of 8 to 11 reading and hearing sentences word by word, those with high digit spans slowed more than those with low spans on entering a relative clause (Booth et al., 2000). Partly because the premise may not hold uniformly across the languages in this sample, the interaction is also reported separately by language.

The direct evidence does not decide between the accounts. The largest eye-movement study at these ages, with 81 children reading 12 items, found no attenuation (Wonnacott et al., 2016). It manipulated temporary ambiguity, which indexes reanalysis, a different demand from the sustained maintenance that dependency length imposes, so its null result does not transfer to the present operationalisation. Where an interaction has been reported, it was confined to late measures, in a study with 14 children per group (H. S. S. L. Joseph & Liversedge, 2013). Recomputed from that study’s tables as a proportion of baseline, the effects on two of its measures disagree. On go-past time in the target region, our computation gives 15 per cent in the younger children, 25 per cent in the older and 31 per cent in adults. That rise with age is the pattern a positive interaction would produce on a proportional scale. On post-target total reading time, where the study’s own significant interaction lay, the proportional effect ran the other way. It was 27 per cent in the younger children, 12 per cent in the older and 4.5 per cent in adults.

A third possibility is that development across these ages is not monotonic. The study of 250 children, the largest existing test, also reported a U-shaped developmental pattern in the online processing of restrictive relative clauses (Booth et al., 2000). Offline comprehension of complex syntax grows most steeply between 5;6 and 7 years (Frizelle et al., 2025), so the younger band falls immediately after the steepest phase. Online processing, however, can go on changing after accuracy has converged. In a spoken sentence-picture matching task with 16 children per age group, Russian-speaking children of 6 to 7 were less accurate than those of 8 to 10 and adults on every sentence type (Kruchinina et al., 2026). Unlike adults, they showed at most a partial eye-movement signature of revision at the passive participle. Children of 8 to 10 matched adults on accuracy and showed the adults’ signature, yet still fixated the target picture less often while hearing the verb. An age difference that accuracy no longer registers may therefore persist in reading pace.

Hypothesis 3: referential continuity and the age band. We register the ReferentialContinuity × AgeBand interaction as a second two-sided adjudicating test, because theory supplies two moderators of coherence facilitation that push the interaction in opposite directions. They are background knowledge and knowledge of the cue, each rising between 7 and 11. The first derives from the construction-integration model (Kintsch, 1988), and the second from what exploiting a cohesive cue requires. On the knowledge account, a gap in the text is bridged from knowledge that the younger reader has less of and retrieves less readily. Age differences in coherence inferencing between 6 and 15 persisted when the relevant knowledge was made equally available to every child, and its accessibility mattered more for younger children (Barnes et al., 1996). At 11 to 12, higher knowledge went with faster reading of sentences that required an inference (Cain et al., 2024). Surface continuity substitutes for an inference that is costlier at 7 to 8 than at 9 and over, so it is worth more to the younger reader and the interaction is positive.

On the cue-utilisation account, exploiting a connective requires enough decoding fluency to reach it and enough explicit knowledge of what it signals, so the older reader gains more and the interaction is negative. Knowledge of causal and adversative conjunctions is still developing between 8 and 10 (Cain et al., 2005; Cain & Nash, 2011), and comprehension of connectives grows across the primary years (Volodina & Weinert, 2020). Like the capacity account, the knowledge account is the theory object’s own proposition, with the cue-utilisation account registered there as its rival, so a negative interaction would count against that proposition.

The cue-utilisation account depends on decoding fluency, so it implies a larger negative interaction where decoding is still maturing across the two bands, which the orthographic-depth paragraph below places in English, French and Portuguese. The knowledge account, resting on background knowledge and narrative experience, implies no such dependence. The per-language interactions registered under Sensitivity analyses are reported partly for that reason.

Decoding fluency and knowledge of the cue develop well past the ages studied here. Knowledge of what a connective signals is still incomplete in French-speaking teenagers of 12 to 19 and native Russian-speaking teenagers of 11 to 17. In both samples, vocabulary size and exposure to print were stronger correlates of appropriate use than age (Tskhovrebova et al., 2022, 2023). Both moderators are likely to grow with the reading a child accumulates, a further reason the net sign of the interaction cannot be derived in advance.

The two accounts concern different anchors of ReferentialContinuity. On the knowledge account, referential overlap and semantic similarity between adjacent sentences spare the reader a bridging inference, whereas the cue-utilisation account relies on connectives. In adults, semantic and causal relatedness dissociate by reader skill, with semantic relatedness weighing more for the less skilled reader (Todaro et al., 2010). They also bear differently on processing time and on what is remembered (Wolfe et al., 2005). The anchors need not therefore show the same age interaction, and a component interaction inside the region could arise from anchor interactions of opposite sign. Where the component posterior lies inside the region while the anchor interactions registered under Sensitivity analyses lie outside it with opposite signs, the equivalence verdict is retained and reported with that pattern. The sign of a coherence effect also depends on the cohesive device involved, so connectives enter the component by relation type, and the causal and contrastive densities are its connective anchors. The facilitation a connective affords was smaller for result relations than for concessive ones and differed between an analytic and a synthetic language (Marchal et al., 2025), which supports both halves of that decision.

A negative interaction has a second interpretation under the Landscape model, which makes a reader’s attempt at a bridging inference depend on the standard of coherence they bring to the text (van den Broek et al., 2005; van den Broek & Helder, 2017). A reader who does not attempt it pays no time for the gap, so continuity would save the younger reader nothing if that band read to a lower standard. The online evidence limits that concern, since children of 8 to 13 made the local inferences a coherent representation required, if not the optional ones (H. Joseph et al., 2021). This design does not separate a lower standard of coherence from cue utilisation, so Table 1 treats a negative verdict as consistent with both.

As with Hypothesis 2, the existing evidence does not decide between the accounts. Eight- and ten-year-olds gained the same speed-up from an appropriate connective in self-paced reading, with the age interaction explicitly null across 91 children (Cain & Nash, 2011). No difference in online sensitivity to a coherence break was found between children of 8 to 9 and of 10 to 11 (Helder et al., 2016). Among 8- to 10-year-olds reading two-clause sentences, the absence of the Dutch causal connective lengthened processing only for second-language readers with weaker syntactic knowledge (Van den Bosch et al., 2018). The connective was worth most to the reader with the fewest resources, but the contrast between language backgrounds leaves age untested. In fourth graders, the effect of cohesion turned on world knowledge and far less on decoding skill, with age untestable within a single grade (McNamara et al., 2011). Where the studies we found at these ages manipulated whole-text coherence, they measured comprehension or inference, so none bears directly on reading rate. The three outcomes are interpreted as for Hypothesis 2.

The bottleneck-release account links the two age-band interactions. The smaller base that raises the proportional grammatical cost in the older band also enlarges the proportional continuity saving there. The account predicts a positive Hypothesis 2 interaction, a negative Hypothesis 3 interaction and a negative or null LexicalDifficulty × AgeBand interaction. Differential attrition and a larger non-reading share in the younger band, both described above, would produce the same signs for Hypotheses 2 and 3. Each, however, would push the lexical interaction positive, the one sign that bottleneck release excludes. We will report the joint pattern of these three tier-1 estimates descriptively, and no verdict depends on it.

Both registered age-band interactions are averaged over languages whose writing systems differ in type and in depth, and the age band does not mean the same thing in each. In most European orthographies, children read familiar words accurately and fluently within the first school year, whereas development is more than twice as slow in English and lags in French and Portuguese (Seymour et al., 2003). Psycholinguistic grain size theory derives that pattern from how consistently a writing system represents phonology, which sets the size of the units a learner comes to rely on (Ziegler & Goswami, 2005). Children matched on years of instruction show orthography-specific length and frequency signatures, with English qualitatively unlike German and Finnish, while adults converge (Schroeder et al., 2022). Between two transparent orthographies, the developmental trajectory can nonetheless be comparable, with language-specific effects confined to morphological complexity (Marzi et al., 2026), so the heterogeneity expected here is a matter of degree. Children reading different orthographies are therefore at different points on the automatisation curve. On the bottleneck-release account, the developmental signature would be largest where decoding automatises latest. The registered coefficient is a prevalence-weighted average across those positions, and we do not generalise it from any one orthography (Share, 2008).

Hypothesis 4: narration and selective attenuation. We predict that the LexicalDifficulty coefficient will be proportionally smaller under narration than the ReferentialContinuity coefficient is. With \(\pi_{\text{lex}}\) and \(\pi_{\text{cont}}\) denoting each coefficient’s proportional attenuation under narration, computed draw by draw as set out under Confirmatory models, the prediction is that \(\pi_{\text{lex}} - \pi_{\text{cont}} > 0.20\). The threshold is four times the region’s half-width and is a registered convention. The prediction follows from the decoding-scaffolding account. A spoken rendition supplies word identity without print-to-sound mapping, the step that a low-frequency or long word makes expensive. The rendition does not supply the link between two ideas, which the reader must still construct. The benefit of text-to-speech is largest for children whose decoding is weak but whose language is intact (Keelor et al., 2023). In 34 children aged 8 to 9, the time saved by the audio fell on the first encounter with an orthographically novel word and had gone by the second (A. Valentini et al., 2024). Besides word identity, a voice conveys prosodic phrasing, which a silent reader must project (Breen, 2014) and which is still developing across the primary years (Godde et al., 2020). Narration could thereby lower the grammatical coefficient by a route unrelated to word identity. Prosodic phrasing does not, however, supply the link between two sentences, so the contrast between the lexical and continuity attenuations remains interpretable. The grammatical-by-narration interaction is reported for description only.

Whether narration spares the reader the decoding cost depends on timing. In reading while listening, adults’ eyes generally ran ahead of the audio and rarely rested on the word being heard (Conklin et al., 2020). Among 119 learners of English, the multimodal condition ran slightly slower than reading alone (Malone et al., 2025), whereas children of 8 to 9 showed the opposite pattern (A. Valentini et al., 2024). The decoding account describes a reader slower than the audio, and the competing audio-pacing account a reader faster than it. The full-sample contrast shows which kind of reader predominates in the narrated sessions.

On the pacing account, the audio’s duration sets narrated reading time, so each narrated slope converges on the slope of audio duration per word. That slope is near zero for referential continuity and positive for lexical properties tied to word length, since a word with more syllables takes longer to speak. The two attenuations are therefore equal only once that duration is accounted for, and otherwise the pacing account predicts \(\pi_{\text{lex}} - \pi_{\text{cont}}\) at or below zero. Reading aloud runs at about 183 words per minute, against 238 to 260 for silent reading, and the gap between text types is predicted by word length (Brysbaert, 2019). Pacing alone fails to flatten word-level effects, since manipulating reading speed left the influence of word properties on eye movements in place (Schwalm et al., 2026). That result partly motivates the registered contrast between the lexical and continuity attenuations.

The compression diagnostic withholds the full-sample verdict where the audio sets the pace for most narrated sessions. An adjustment for the audio’s duration allows for the mixture that the diagnostic can miss, in which only some narrated sessions are paced. The adjustment rests on a predicted spoken duration per word, defined under Materials and computed from the texts before any outcome is linked to them. The refit including it is registered under Sensitivity analyses.

The pacing account has substantial support. The meta-analytic comprehension benefit reported above is confined to comparisons with experimenter-paced reading (Clinton-Lisell, 2023). A registered report testing the resource-freeing mechanism in 86 adult second-language readers found comprehension worse under narration than in silent reading (Hui & Godfroid, 2026). In the same study, individual differences in orthographic decoding and speech segmentation did not interact with input condition in the way that mechanism predicts. In 21 secondary-school students with dyslexia and 22 without, audio support increased reading time in most tasks while leaving comprehension unchanged (Knoop-van Campen et al., 2021). Where measured incidentally, the compression is severe, with the standard deviation of exposure time falling from 11.56 seconds when self-paced to 0.62 seconds when narrated (A. Valentini et al., 2024). The tests we found concern comprehension and the variance of exposure time, and none bears on the contrast that Hypothesis 4 registers.

A third possibility, with the same predictions as the pacing account, is that the child listens without reading, so that the interval is the audio’s duration plus the page turns. Attention to print is low in preschoolers listening to a storybook (Evans & Saint-Aubin, 2005) and rises across the primary grades with reading skill (Roy-Charland et al., 2007). In older children reading an illustrated story in a second language, audio moved the eyes from the text to the pictures without changing comprehension (Pellicer-Sánchez et al., 2020; Serrano & Pellicer-Sánchez, 2022). By contrast, 8- to 9-year-olds did fixate the words under bimodal presentation (A. Valentini et al., 2024). The share of narrated sessions in which the child read along is unknown and likely to differ by age band. The logs cannot separate a paced reading from a listening. An inside-region verdict is therefore reported as time set by the audio, whether or not the child read along, and never as evidence that the narrated sessions involved reading. Listening without reading is ruled out by definition only in the narrated sessions that the audio cannot have paced, the unpaced subset defined under Confirmatory models.

The flag records the voice setting at the end of a session (question (a) in Table 2). A narrated session is therefore one in which narration was available, not necessarily one in which the child listened, and a setting changed during the session is recorded only in its final state. Under the rule registered for that answer, the Hypothesis 4 contrast is an intention-to-treat comparison. Sessions misclassified in this way, and children who ignored the audio, dilute both attenuations towards zero, and the dilution is stated wherever the coefficient is reported.

The Hypothesis 4 contrast is also exposed to selection into narration state. With narration on in about nine sessions in ten, the unnarrated slopes, the denominators of both attenuation proportions, are estimated largely from the families who chose to switch the audio off. If the children reading without audio are disproportionately the fluent readers, their slopes are attenuated by selection and both proportions are distorted. The data contain no measure of reading skill with which to adjust for this, so we will report narration prevalence by age band, reading level and language before fitting. The rule under Confirmatory models makes the within-account contrast the primary estimate where the data allow. Because both proportions are ratios, the contrast is evaluated only under the Hypothesis 4 precondition, that fewer than 5 per cent of the draws of each unnarrated slope, lexical and continuity, lie inside the region. If either slope fails the precondition, there is no attenuation to compare and Hypothesis 4 is reported as not evaluable. The precondition does not depend on the sign of any coefficient.

Hypothesis 5: text properties and the difficulty rating. We predict that, in an ordinal model of the post-reading rating, LexicalDifficulty and GrammaticalLoad will be associated with higher odds of a harder rating and ReferentialContinuity with lower odds, each by an odds ratio outside the region. We further predict that the three standardised coefficients will rank by magnitude as they do in the reading-time model. Two measures of the same three constructs, one behavioural and one judgement-based, obtained on different scales from overlapping sets of children, need not rank those constructs the same way. Their agreement on order is therefore a substantive prediction of the theory object, on which both measures record the same expended effort.

The link between the two measures rests on the effort heuristic of Koriat’s cue-utilisation approach to metacognitive judgement. On that approach, a judgement of difficulty is inferred from cues such as experienced effort and does not directly reflect processing (Koriat, 1997; Undorf & Erdfelder, 2011). Two meta-analyses put the association between experienced effort and monitoring judgements at r = -.36 and, in a meta-analytic structural model, at \(\beta\) = -.19 (Baars et al., 2020; David et al., 2024). Those are judgements of learning and of confidence, so for a difficulty rating the sign is reversed. Second, third and fifth graders were less confident in an answer the longer they had taken to choose it, and reliance on that cue was stronger in the older children (Koriat & Ackerman, 2010). The rating would then be a later report of the same expended effort that reading time measures, not a separate measurement of the text. Effort is attributed to the material when the material imposes it, but not when a learner chooses to invest it, a distinction established in adult learners (Koriat et al., 2006; Koriat et al., 2014). In children, consistent with that distinction, the association between effort and judgement weakens where regulation is goal driven (Baars et al., 2020).

Partly because reading in this app is self-paced and voluntary, the rating is registered here as a measure under test. On the same grounds, the theory object marks this prediction as diagnostic against the engagement account. Convergence in the predicted directions weighs against that account. Its absence is interpreted, under the rule in Table 1, as a failure of the rating and not as support for the engagement account.

That approach also suggests why the two measures might diverge. A judgement made after finishing draws on the cues still accessible at that moment, and the three sources of demand are not equally accessible. Word-level difficulty recurs at every line, whereas the cost of an unstated link between ideas is spread over the text. Consistent with this, a cohesion manipulation that moved paragraph-level judgements left whole-text judgements untouched unless readers could still consult the text (Lefèvre & Lories, 2004). In adults predicting their own recall, however, judgements about a whole text did rise with coherence (Rawson & Dunlosky, 2002). The posterior probability that the two measures rank the components alike is the study’s principal convergent-validity check, evaluated against the 0.90 criterion under Decision rules.

The evidence closest to this rating concerns related judgements, mostly those children make about their own learning and comprehension. Among children of 8 to 10 solving arithmetic problems, the mental effort they reported investing was related to their judgements of learning, which tracked the complexity of the problem (Baars et al., 2018). Children in both of this study’s age bands judge how well they have understood a text (Steiner et al., 2020), and fifth graders do so both for the whole text and for individual answers (Markovich et al., 2026). Primary-school children are more overconfident in those judgements than secondary-school children (Mirandola et al., 2018), but that overconfidence depends on the text. Among 407 primary and secondary students, the primary children overestimated their performance on expository passages and inferential questions but calibrated as well as the older students on narrative texts (Zagato et al., 2026). Every text in this study is narrative. Comprehension judgements concern the reader’s own understanding, whereas the rating here asks about the text. In adults, subjective comprehensibility ratings of narrative text can be predicted from computed text features (Tilmatine et al., 2024). None of the child metacognition literature we found validates a global post-reading judgement of a text’s difficulty against the text’s measured properties in children of this age. We likewise found no psychometric evaluation of the three-point format, a classroom heuristic whose options are easy, just right and difficult.

Children’s monitoring judgements are internally consistent when aggregated over items, their reliability is limited by that of the first-order task, and their accuracy varies from one task to another (Eberhart et al., 2025; Roebers et al., 2021). The design therefore aggregates ratings at text level and applies the five-rater floor registered under Confirmatory models. Children of 6 to 13 have a limited grasp of multi-point response formats, and word-anchored options agree better with a yes-or-no standard than numeric ones do (Mellor & Moore, 2014). The number of options bears on how reliably children and young adolescents answer, with four options proving optimal and seven or more lowering reliability (Borgers et al., 2004). Which way any of this would move a three-point rating inside an app is not established, so we register the direction as unknown and report the observed distribution. Equating reported difficulty with expended effort is contestable. In 80 children aged 8 to 12, the difficulty and effort ratings given after a battery of cognitive tasks were uncorrelated with each other and only weakly related to performance (Torres & Toplak, 2026).

With age, children come to judge a task by the effort it cost. In self-paced learning, judgements of learning fell as study time rose for third, fifth and sixth graders (mean ages 8.9 to 12.0) but not for first and second graders (mean ages 7.0 and 7.9) (Koriat et al., 2009). The authors place that division at about 8.5 years, near this study’s age-band boundary, but their finding does not show whether direct judgements of a text divide the same way. A rating that tracks the text at 7 to 8 remains possible, because even 5- and 6-year-olds use an item’s difficulty adaptively when evaluating their own performance (van Loon et al., 2017). The rating model includes the same component-by-age-band terms as the reading-time model, but its registered tests remain the pooled odds ratios and the ordering. The band-specific odds ratios derived from those terms are descriptive, as stated under Confirmatory models. Recorded age bands in the structural sample skewed towards the younger band (Supplementary Section S8), so a prevalence-weighted pooled odds ratio will be weighted towards that band. Table 1 accordingly interprets a pooled odds ratio inside the region, together with an older-band odds ratio outside it, as a limit on the rating specific to the younger band.

The effective sample size is the number of distinct children rating each text, which the raw count of ratings overstates, so the model has a varying intercept for Text. We will report the intraclass correlation for the rating at text level as a reliability estimate for this format. Before fitting, we will report the distribution of raters per text and the distribution of the three rating categories overall and by age band and language. The category distribution will be reported again alongside any inside-region verdict, because a distribution concentrated in one category limits what a cumulative logit can resolve.

A failure here is ambiguous on its own, since it could lie in the rating or in the components. Because Hypothesis 1 resolves the ambiguity, the interpretations in Table 1 are conditioned on the reading-time coefficients. Components that predict reading time but not the rating leave the feature pipeline sound. Partial convergence in Table 1, with directions as predicted but an ordering probability below 0.90, does not call the components into question. The design analysis marks this hypothesis as the one most likely to be underpowered. If it is declared underpowered, or its verdict is inconclusive, the ordering is reported descriptively and convergent validity is recorded as not established. This rule gives the fifth outcome-neutral check a defined outcome whether or not Hypothesis 5 reaches a verdict. An inconclusive verdict from the census of existing records is still informative, because it sets an upper limit on the reliability a single-item difficulty rating can have at the availability such apps achieve.

Hypothesis 6: accumulated reading and reading pace. We predict that, among children with several recorded sessions, a one-standard-deviation within-child increase in accumulated in-app reading, on its log scale, will be associated with a reduction in log reading time per word. The reduction is predicted over and above session order, the interval since the previous session and the time since the child’s first session. Measures defines the within-child standard deviation, the unit for which the region under Decision rules is stated. For each language, we will report the change implied by a doubling of one plus the accumulated stories. It equals the coefficient multiplied by \(\ln 2\) and divided by that language’s within-child standard deviation of accumulated reading.

On the orthographic-learning account, each successful encounter with a written word form is an opportunity to acquire or sharpen the word-specific orthographic representation supporting its later recognition (Share, 1995). Under the Lexical Quality Hypothesis, the quality of those representations makes skilled word reading cheap (Perfetti, 2007). Print experience is accordingly treated as the main source of growth in word reading (Castles et al., 2018; Deacon et al., 2024; Nation, 2017). Because the mechanism operates on items, exposure is counted in words of completed distinct stories, and repeated readings are reserved for the practice analysis under Sensitivity analyses. Each further form adds less as the lexicon fills, so accumulated reading enters on a log scale.

Learning of this kind is primarily item-based. In grades 1 and 2, at or just below the younger band, children who met novel spellings in short stories learned them, but transferred that learning clearly only to orthographically related forms (Tucker & Deacon, 2025). Across 62 experiments, the outcome of such learning is modulated by language-specific word properties (Li & Wang, 2023). Reading one story can make the next cheaper by two routes, the words the two texts share and the sublexical regularities a reader generalises from them (Castles et al., 2018). The size of the association is therefore limited by how much of a new text earlier reading can already have made easier. Both limits are looser for stories written to one reading level than for texts sampled at large, and neither is measured here.

Outside English, orthographic learning, lexical knowledge, reading fluency and spelling were associated in German-speaking primary-school children (Schmalz et al., 2025). The account’s implications differ between languages, because decoding automatises at very different ages across European orthographies (Seymour et al., 2003), as the paragraph on orthographic depth above records. On a model in which decipherability precedes unitisation and automatisation (Share, 2025), the per-unit speed-up that accumulated reading affords should be shallower where decoding was automatised earlier. The pooled deviation coefficient remains the registered test. The per-language slopes from the secondary refit under Sensitivity analyses will be reported descriptively alongside it, annotated with orthographic depth. Seymour et al. (2003) place eight of the app’s languages on five ordered levels of depth but omit the other eleven. Polish will be annotated at the middle level, beside Dutch and Portuguese (Awramiuk & Krasowicz-Kupis, 2013; Schüppert et al., 2017), and Korean as shallow in the reading direction (Leachman et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2023). Indonesian, Romanian and Turkish will be annotated as shallow, and Russian, like Korean, as shallow in the reading direction (Borleffs et al., 2017; Dolean et al., 2021; Rakhlin et al., 2014; Siegelman et al., 2022). Arabic, Hebrew, Thai and Chinese are annotated by type of writing system, since a scale built to order European alphabets does not order them. Supplementary Section S3 gives the evidence for every placement, all of them fixed with the feature inventory.

As noted earlier, practice and skill are reciprocal over years (Torppa et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2021), so the coefficient estimates a within-window association, not a practice effect. Random-intercept cross-lagged models on more than 5,100 German secondary students found no within-person evidence that reading more yields better reading competence, while the reverse path, from competence to exposure, remained (Bittmann, 2025). Only a design that separates within-person change from stable between-person differences can show such an asymmetry. The within-child deviation and the account-mean term separate them by definition. For the path from reading to skill, exposure to a text experimentally speeded skilled adults’ later recognition of the low-frequency words in it (Chetail, 2024). That study, the closest experimental demonstration we found, is confined to adults and to rare words. Reciprocity established at yearly lags does not by itself license treating the reverse path as negligible over a window of months.

Within the window, a child whose session went slowly may not return, so later sessions would over-represent children whose earlier sessions went well, which would push the coefficient in the predicted direction. The family of a child reading faster may choose longer stories or raise the reading level. Either change alters the composition of what accumulates, even though the current story’s length and level are adjusted for. Before the coefficient is interpreted, we will report the distribution of each user account’s final-session pace relative to that account’s own mean, and the number of accounts whose reading level changed within the window. The audit under Sampling plan establishes that number in any case. Two refits will be reported with the registered term, and no verdict depends on either. One will exclude every account’s final eligible session, and the other will add the previous session’s standardised pace as a covariate, by analogy with the random-intercept cross-lagged model (Hamaker et al., 2015). Because conditioning on a lagged outcome alongside an Account intercept is biased in short series, the lagged-pace refit constrains the size of the concern without resolving it.

The account-mean term is reported, but it tests neither the orthographic-learning account nor its reverse-path rival. Both predict it negative, since a child who has read more in the app may read faster for having done so and a child who reads faster may go on reading. How much and how well children read have long been associated at the between-person level (Mol & Bus, 2011). Only the within-child deviation separates the two accounts, and the rival is substantive. In three twin samples, the modelled direction of causation ran from reading ability, literacy skill or comprehension to print exposure or literacy enjoyment, with the reverse influence zero in one sample (Erbeli et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2018; van Bergen et al., 2023).

Each covariate addresses a named alternative. Session order absorbs familiarity with the interface and with the generator’s story schemas, since a reader who knows the shape of a story reads it faster for reasons the orthographic-learning account does not claim. Days since the previous session absorbs spacing, and days since the first session absorbs maturation and schooling. Once the three are held constant, with session order entered as its logarithm under Measures, the accumulated-reading deviation is identified by within-child variation in the lengths of the stories already read. That variation is small where the reading level fixes story length. Partly for that reason, Design and precision analysis re-derives the tier-2 requirement before fitting, at the observed distribution of sessions per child and with the three covariates added. Oral reading fluency grows appreciably and unevenly over a school year (Christ et al., 2010), so an elapsed-time covariate absorbs maturation only if growth is close to linear in days within a child and the window’s position in the school year is immaterial. Growth rates differ between children, and the variables that predict those rates (Khanolainen et al., 2024) predict how much a child reads as well. Before Hypothesis 6 is interpreted, we will report the within-child correlation matrix of the three covariates and the deviation term, with their variance inflation factors. At the same stage, we will report the calendar month of each session as a descriptive stratifier, to identify any window spanning a school holiday. Table 1 gives the interpretation of each outcome for the deviation term.

Every hypothesis is labelled by the rules under Decision rules, and an inconclusive result is reported as such. No hypothesis is filtered by a preliminary significance test, but three labels take precedence over a verdict. A hypothesis is declared untestable if its component fails the construction rule under Outcome-blind dimension reduction or if its outcome or contrast is absent from the frozen extract. Hypothesis 6 is also declared untestable if account identifiers in the transferred extract remain in floating-point notation, as set out under Data provenance. That label is assigned before the model is fitted and without reference to any coefficient. A hypothesis whose data fall below its precision threshold in Table 1, as re-derived where Design and precision analysis says so, is instead declared underpowered. Its model is still fitted and reported, and no conclusion is drawn from an interval the design analysis predicted would be indeterminate. A hypothesis is reported as not evaluable when a registered post-fitting contingency removes its test. Only two contingencies do so: failure of the Hypothesis 4 precondition and failure of the positive control on recorded reading time. Where the data controller’s answers to the questions in Table 2 falsify an auxiliary assumption, the affected predictions are reassigned by the rules in that table, never reinterpreted after the fact.

Whatever the verdicts, this study will not identify a causal effect of any text property, age band or narration setting. Nor will it estimate how fast children in general read, or generalise beyond families who chose to install and use this application. Its confirmatory results will say nothing about comprehension, since none of the contingent hypotheses is activated at submission. The constraints on generality are these: children aged 7 and over who read narrative texts of a few hundred words on a touchscreen, in one of the app’s 19 languages, inside one commercial application (Simons et al., 2017). One generator wrote the generated stories in English, and machine translation rendered them in every other language. Outside English, the generated texts are therefore machine-translated prose, and the associations estimated there are associations with such texts.

The medium is one of those constraints, and it is not neutral. Reading on screen yields poorer comprehension than reading the same text on paper, with the shortfall larger under time pressure and for informational text (Delgado et al., 2018). In children, the paper advantage holds except where a digital text adds features aligned with the content (Furenes et al., 2021). A network meta-analysis of 56 samples locates most of that shortfall in scrolling (Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger, 2026). Where scrolling was required, paper outperformed every device by 0.35 to 0.48 standard deviations. Where it was not, the differences ran from 0.03 to 0.12 and were unreliable, and a tablet read without scrolling ranked closest to paper (Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger, 2026). This app presents a story as pages on a tablet, so the largest known moderator of the screen shortfall does not apply here. The laboratory results underlying the three components were obtained largely on paper, so the comparison drawn here spans a change of medium as well as of setting. A coefficient smaller than its laboratory counterpart may reflect either change.

Design

Data provenance, sequestration and outcome blindness

The data this protocol analyses already exist. Nature Human Behaviour considers secondary analyses of existing data on condition that authors can evidence no prior access to the data in question. We put the arrangement set out below to the journal’s editorial office, which confirmed it as appropriate on 3 August 2026.

The data controller builds the extract on its own schedule and holds it. No part of the extract will be transferred to us until the journal confirms in-principle acceptance in writing. Until then, and with the editorial office’s agreement, three items may be sent to us: a manifest, the story texts and the provider-computed counts. The manifest is the record that the count script described below keeps of each file it reads: the file’s name, size, column list, row count, range of session dates and SHA-256 checksum. With it, the file eventually transferred can be shown to be the one that was built. The certification in Supplementary Section S7 states the build date and the window the extract covers. The story texts are identified by story identifier, reading language, reading level and app version, and their page breaks are preserved. They may be received before in-principle acceptance because every linguistic measure is computed on them and none contains a behavioural outcome.

The provider-computed counts are aggregates that the data controller obtains from the extract by running a count script we supplied. The count script reads nineteen columns besides reading time, writes one small table of counts and a readable web page drawn from it, and never reports a reading time as a value. Both the version sent on 7 August 2026 and a revised version, sent on 18 September 2026, are deposited with this protocol. The revision adds checks against malformed input, among them a count of identifiers in floating-point notation, and extends the first version’s record of each file read. On the structural sample and on clean synthetic exports, it reproduces every count of the first version. The two differ where the first version misread an input, for example by filing a session under its story title when the story identifier was empty. The data controller has been asked to run the revised version, because two rules below use its check of the account identifiers.

A count computed from an outcome column could compromise blindness, so the script treats reading time only as a column whose usability has to be established. It reports how many values are absent, non-numeric, negative or zero, how many fall into each of seven bands bounded by successive powers of ten, and how many distinct values the column contains. The script also reports the largest number of sessions sharing any single value, and it never crosses reading time with language, age, text, account or date. Supplementary Table 6 shows its output on the structural sample. Bands this coarse cannot distinguish a fast text from a slow one, but they do distinguish a column recorded in seconds from one recorded in milliseconds. The analysis cannot be specified until the column’s unit is known. A session-timeout cap would appear as a large number of sessions sharing one value, and the censoring rule under Sampling plan depends on that count.

In the structural sample, account identifiers were written in floating-point notation with six significant digits (Supplementary Section S8), a form in which distinct accounts can share a value. The revised script therefore counts the sessions whose account or story identifier is still in that notation. If any account identifier is in that notation, the script marks the account figures as unreliable and asks for the identifiers to be re-exported as integers or as text. If identifiers in that notation remain in the transferred extract, accounts cannot be told apart reliably, and neither tier 2 nor the within-account narration contrast can be formed. Hypothesis 6 is then declared untestable, and no other analysis of tier 2 is run. Tier 1 is still analysed, without the Account intercept, as when the Account rule under Sampling plan is not met. The restriction to first readings and the five-rater floor are applied to the identifiers as recorded.

Materials

The reading materials are the story texts, each written by a generative model around the combination of five attributes a family chooses. Families may also choose a story from a classic set. For each reading, the extract records whether the text was generated or classic, the reading level served, the language, the app version and the page count. Every session will be linked to the features of the exact version it was served, matched on story identifier, reading language, reading level and app version.

Every text coefficient is identified by comparing texts, so how much this design can establish depends on the number of distinct texts with eligible readings, which is not yet known. The data controller’s estimate spans a few hundred to a few million, depending on whether a combination of attributes yields one shared text or a new text for each session. The evidence favours shared texts. The platform’s published description reports a catalogue of over 40,000 generated English-language stories, many of them translated into the child’s home language (Ratner et al., 2025). In the structural sample, the story identifiers of two attribute combinations recurred across 11 and 7 of the 35 accounts (Supplementary Section S8). A recurring identifier cannot show whether the text behind it was regenerated, however, so we put question (d) in Table 2 to the data controller. Its answer, received in October 2026, is that each story is generated once in English, translated into the other languages by machine translation and stored, and that every reader of a given version sees the same text. Under the rule the table registers for that answer, the models include a varying intercept for the underlying story, crossed with Language.

For every text, we will compute features in four families. Lexical features are mean log frequency of content words, the proportion of words with a frequency below one per million tokens on the uniform frequency resource, mean rated age of acquisition and mean orthographic length. Mean per-word surprisal under a language-specific neural language model, lexical density, lexical diversity and concreteness complete the family. Diversity is the moving-average type-token ratio over a 50-token window, and its per-language correlation with the Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity will be reported. A word’s surprisal is the sum of the surprisals of the subword tokens composing it, and the mean is taken over the same whitespace-delimited words as the outcome’s denominator. Grammatical features are mean dependency distance from a universal-dependencies parse, clauses per sentence, the ratio of subordinate to main clauses, mean sentence length and mean depth of noun-phrase modification. Discourse features are mean semantic similarity between adjacent sentences from a multilingual sentence encoder, referential overlap, connectives per hundred words by relation type and narrativity. Referential overlap is the proportion of adjacent sentence pairs sharing at least one content lemma in a nominal position, which operationalises the argument-overlap rule of the construction-integration model without a coreference resolver. Narrativity is a stipulated composite of past-tense marking, personal pronouns and direct speech, defined in Supplementary Table 3. Affective features are the mean and the variability of valence and of arousal. One further text quantity serves Hypothesis 4 alone, a predicted spoken duration per word on a log scale, computed from syllables per word plus sentence- and clause-boundary pauses per word from punctuation. Its resources and matched-token proportion are given in Supplementary Table 3. If the data controller supplies the audio’s duration for every eligible text, the logarithm of that duration per word replaces the prediction, because the pacing account concerns the audio’s actual duration. The prediction is then kept for a sensitivity analysis. Durations supplied for only some texts serve a sensitivity analysis restricted to them.

The extract records narration only as a session-level flag. The data controller confirmed in October 2026 that the flag holds the voice setting at the end of the session, so it shows that narration was available, not that it played. The platform has not disclosed how the audio is produced or presented. Two undisclosed properties bear on the interpretation of a narrated session. Word-level highlighting couples the eye to the voice, although the evidence comes from children younger than any here (Liao et al., 2020). A page that advances when playback ends would make a narrated session’s duration largely the audio’s duration. Question (a) in Table 2 lists these and the remaining items with the consequence of each answer. Any item established from the public version of the app is reported as observed, with the date. Observing the app involves no outcome data, and none of these facts enters a model.

Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 give the primary and the sensitivity resource for each feature family, with their coverage across the app’s 19 languages. Four rules govern the tables and are fixed before any feature is computed. First, each feature will be computed from a single resource applied identically in every language, even where a better native resource exists in one of them. Native norms will be run as a pre-specified sensitivity analysis, and their correlation with the single-resource values will be reported for each language. Second, no value is transferred across languages by translating a word list. A predicted resource is admitted only where the prediction was made within each language by one documented procedure and its correlation with native ratings is reported. Where no acceptable measure exists for a language, that language does not contribute that feature.

Third, every measure reports the proportion of tokens it matched, and a language in which a measure matches fewer than 80 per cent of content-word tokens does not contribute it. Content-word tokens are those whose universal part-of-speech tag is NOUN, VERB, ADJ or ADV. The token-coverage rule differs from the text-coverage rule under Outcome-blind dimension reduction, and the two can reach opposite decisions on the same feature. Where some languages lack a feature, that family’s pooled solution is computed on the features common to every contributing language. Such a feature enters only the per-language descriptive solutions and the language-specific sensitivity analyses. The first rule has two exceptions, concreteness and age of acquisition. No uniform concreteness resource meets the second rule, and the one cross-linguistic age-of-acquisition set has only 299 words, too few to yield a text mean. Both are therefore taken from native norms where these meet the third rule, and Outcome-blind dimension reduction sets out how they enter the component solutions.

Fourth, the full inventory, the software versions, the model checkpoints, the tokenisation rules and the matched-token proportions will be frozen and published before any feature is linked to outcome data. Sentence segmentation is registered with them: pages are concatenated with a single space, and a page break is never a sentence boundary. Supplementary Section S3 lists the per-language segmentation diagnostics to be reported. The four rules constrain Korean most, and the same section sets out the consequences for it after Supplementary Table 3.

Proper nouns are excluded from the content-word denominator of the third rule and from every norm-based mean, because the platform puts invented names into every story. The hero, the companion and the setting a family chose are named in the text, and no subtitle frequency list, age-of-acquisition norm or concreteness norm contains an invented name. Those names recur through the story, so we will report three length-normalised name quantities from the same universal-dependencies parse. They are the proportion of tokens tagged as proper nouns, the number of distinct proper-noun types per hundred words and the mean repetitions per type. Each norm-based mean is reported with and without the exclusion, together with the two versions’ correlation within each language. Surprisal is computed with the names present in context and excluded only from the mean.

Measures

The processing outcome, serving Hypotheses 1 to 4 and 6, is the natural logarithm of elapsed reading time divided by the story’s word count, standardised within reading language. Hypothesis 5 has a separate outcome, the child’s optional post-reading rating of the story as easy, just right or difficult, treated as ordered and modelled on a cumulative-logit scale. A word, in the processing outcome’s denominator and in every per-word quantity, is a whitespace-delimited token of the text as served. Chinese and Thai do not separate words with spaces, so in those languages a word is a token of the frozen word segmenter of the pipeline registered for the language in Supplementary Table 4. In most of the other languages, the token is an ordinary word. In Korean, it is an eojeol, a content word with its postpositional particles or verbal endings, which is the unit of the Korean dependency treebanks and of both parsers in Supplementary Table 3. A Korean text therefore contains fewer tokens than a translation of it would, so for the same content a Korean rate in words per minute is lower and not comparable with the other languages. The same holds to a lesser degree in Arabic and Hebrew, which write the article and some prepositions and conjunctions as prefixes, and in Turkish, whose words carry chains of suffixes. Of the plausibility bounds under Sampling plan, the fast one excludes fewer readings in these languages and the slow one more. Associations in each language are interpreted through its own language-varying slopes throughout.

The per-word scale also ties the outcome to word length. Mean orthographic length anchors LexicalDifficulty and helps determine how many words a text contains, since the same content in longer words needs fewer of them. As a result, time per word rises with word length even where time per character is unchanged. The per-character refit under Sensitivity analyses removes that dependence without altering the primary scale. Where the extract includes the platform’s own word count, we will report its agreement with ours before any outcome model is fitted.

The outcome’s intraclass correlations at the Text and the Account level will be reported from the tier-1 variance components. They give the reliability with which a text and an account are characterised by their readings.

The confirmatory predictors are the three component scores defined under Outcome-blind dimension reduction. Text length in words, narrativity, mean valence and mean arousal are pre-specified secondary text predictors and enter every model as covariates. Valence and arousal effects on word-recognition latency are larger among low-frequency words (Kuperman et al., 2014), so the affective means overlap LexicalDifficulty and enter only as adjustments. Omitted, they would leave part of that component’s association unadjusted. No interaction between an affective mean and a component is estimated, and the variance inflation factors under Decision rules will show the overlap. Every affective value used here descends from adult ratings, a limitation recorded in Supplementary Section S3. Accordingly, these covariates are interpreted as properties of the text on an adult referent, not as what a child felt. The variability of valence and of arousal, and the additive and temporal connective densities, will be computed for description only and excluded from the component solution. The generated-or-classic indicator enters as a covariate, and the substantive comparison between generated and classic texts is a contingent hypothesis, as question (j) in Table 2 explains. Because predictors are standardised within language, a pooled coefficient averages slopes expressed in each language’s own standard-deviation units. We will report the raw-scale standard deviation of each component per language so that coefficients can be converted to raw units, and the language-varying slopes summarise the heterogeneity this induces.

The age band is the platform’s record of the child’s age group, reduced to the two bands this study covers. The data controller’s answer to question (h) is that the band is derived from the age a family gave when it created the child’s profile, so it is family-reported and unverified. Unless a family updates it, a band set long before the window may understate the child’s age at the time of reading. An account whose recorded band changes within the window contributes each session under the band recorded for it, and the number of such accounts will be reported. The reading level is the level the family has set for the session, which determines which version of a text is served. Narration is the session-level narration flag, which records whether narration was switched on at the end of the session. Under the rule registered for that answer to question (a), the variable is treated as narration availability, and the Hypothesis 4 contrast is an intention-to-treat comparison.

Accumulated in-app reading, which serves Hypothesis 6, is the cumulative count of words in completed distinct stories the account read before the current session. It is expressed in story units, each story’s word count being divided by the median word count of the distinct texts with eligible readings in its language. Entering it as \(\log(1 + \text{accumulated stories})\) keeps it defined at the first eligible session, where the count is zero. On a raw word count, the logarithm would jump after a child’s first session and change little afterwards. The within-child term would then mostly contrast that session, when the app was still new to the child, with later ones.

The transformed variable is decomposed into the account’s mean and the current session’s deviation from it. Both parts are divided by one constant per language, the root mean square of the deviations over that language’s tier-2 readings. The registered coefficient is then the change in the standardised outcome per within-child standard deviation of accumulated reading, the unit the design analysis simulated (Supplementary Section S5). Scaling a within-person effect by within-person variation keeps differences between children’s means out of its unit (Schuurman et al., 2016). The constant is pooled over children because standardising by each child’s own standard deviation needs far more readings than the three that admit a child to tier 2 (L. Wang et al., 2019). With both parts on the same scale, the difference between their coefficients remains a contextual effect. The logarithm of session order, the days since the account’s previous eligible session and the days since its first eligible session enter the tier-2 model with the two terms.

The data are crossed: each reading belongs to one account, but a text is met by many accounts and exists in several languages and at several reading levels. An account may read in more than one language. We will treat Account, Text, Language, Country and App version as crossed grouping factors and report the counts supporting each before fitting any model. The grouping-factor rule admits a factor other than Text and Account when it has at least five levels with at least 20 eligible readings each. Otherwise, the factor is dropped, and the omission will be reported. Reading level, with only three levels, enters as a fixed factor. If at least 90 per cent of texts are read in only one eligible session, a Text varying intercept is not estimable and is replaced by varying intercepts for the repeated attribute combination that generated the text. These thresholds are registered conventions, and any consequence of them will be reported.

Randomisation and blinding

Nothing in this study is randomised, and no allocation, assignment or condition is under anyone’s control.

Data collection and analysis will not be performed blind to the conditions of the study. The platform collects the data without researcher involvement. Before in-principle acceptance, however, we are blind to the outcome in the extract, which the data controller holds as described under Data provenance, sequestration and outcome blindness. After transfer, analysis will not be blind to the values of the predictors or grouping variables, since the models require them. We will write, run and debug the outcome-model code against data simulated from the design specification deposited with this protocol, so that every script is complete and tested before we receive the real extract.

Sampling plan

The sample is a census. We will analyse every eligible record in one frozen extract, whose closing date is fixed before any outcome model is fitted, with no sequential testing or optional stopping.

We will first exclude sessions the platform flags as incomplete or crashed, duplicated event records, accounts the platform flags as duplicates and stories without a positive word count.

Two nested samples are drawn from the remaining records, tier 1 for the text hypotheses and tier 2 for the within-child hypothesis, because these hypotheses are identified at different levels. Tier 1 comprises every completed reading whose account is recorded in either age band (7 to 8, or 9 and over) and whose reading language the feature pipeline covers. The story text must have been supplied, and the reading time must fall inside the plausibility bounds below. Where an account read the same text more than once, only the first reading enters tier 1. Repeated readings are retained for the practice analysis under Sensitivity analyses. The unit of analysis is one reading of one text by one account.

Under the Account rule, Account receives a varying intercept if at least 10 per cent of tier-1 readings come from accounts contributing two or more readings. If fewer do, the intercept cannot be separated from residual variance and is dropped. The sample’s reduced effective independence is then stated with every tier-1 interval. The threshold is a registered convention. It is applied before anything is fitted, to the tier-1 sample as counted in the audit of the frozen extract. The provider-computed counts of sessions per account are not used for it, because they apply no tier-1 exclusion except the restriction to the two age bands.

Tier 2 comprises the tier-1 accounts contributing at least three eligible readings of distinct texts. Three is the smallest number that leaves a degree of freedom once a within-child slope and an intercept are estimated.

The plausibility bounds on reading time per word are set by what a child could physically be doing, not by these data. We will exclude a reading faster than 400 words per minute (0.15 seconds per word) as too fast to be reading. Silent reading in educated adults typically runs between 200 and 400 words per minute (Rayner et al., 2016), so a child of 7 to 11 sustaining more than 400 is not plausibly reading every word. A meta-analysis of Spanish reading rates applied the same cut, treating rates above it as a sign that the reader had skipped part of the text. It removed 313 cases, all of them silent-reading rates, from tests that assessed 19,237 participants (Ripoll Salceda et al., 2020). A reading slower than 10 words per minute (six seconds per word) will be excluded as a story left open. The slow bound lies below the meta-analysis’s slowest pooled mean, 30 words per minute for Spanish first graders reading silently, though above the normal-range floor proposed for that grade, 8 words per minute. We will report the number and proportion removed under each rule, by language, age band, reading level and narration state.

If the provider-computed counts show more than 1 per cent of sessions sharing one value of recorded reading time, we will locate that value in the transferred extract before fitting any outcome model. We will treat it as a right-censoring point if it lies in the upper decile of recorded reading times. If so, the reading-time models will use a censored lognormal likelihood, with those sessions retained as censored.

No excluded datum is replaced. A participant-flow diagram will report every exclusion and the retained accounts, readings and texts, overall and by language.

Measurement, availability and missingness audit

Digital traces have an error taxonomy distinct from that of surveys (Sen et al., 2021), and four concerns here fall under the types it names. Three are trace measurement errors, in which the platform’s record is an imperfect proxy for the construct. They concern the age-band and reading-level categories, instrument drift as the app changes version and a reading-time interval that is not fully documented, the subject of question (c) in Table 2. That drift is also a change in platform affordances, which the taxonomy treats as a separate type. The fourth concern is a trace selection error, since a session that is closed or times out before its last page is not in the extract. A completed reading whose recorded time was capped by a timeout is instead censored, and the censoring rule above applies.

Before fitting any outcome model, we will run and archive an audit of the frozen extract and its dictionary. The audit establishes field availability and units, the granularity of the age field, the intervals between an account’s sessions and, crossed with no other field, the proportions of the three rating categories. It records how often texts and reading levels recur, how many accounts change reading level within the window and how the narration flag behaves within accounts and sessions. Its checks cover duplicate and crash flags, changes across app versions, per-language feature coverage and every nesting and crossing count the models require. Missingness will be reported for every planned variable, overall and by language, age band, reading level, narration state and session order.

A feature structurally unavailable in a language is not imputed, so the language does not contribute it. A session missing an outcome is excluded from the model for that outcome alone, and a missing narration flag does not remove a session from a model without a narration term. No variable is selected, transformed or discarded because of its association with reading time or the rating.

The Hypothesis 5 rating is optional and was present in only 10 of the structural sample’s 35 sessions, so we expect high missingness. If the rating is missing for more than 5 per cent of otherwise eligible sessions, we will model its availability from observed pre-response variables. In that case, we will report an inverse-probability-weighted analysis as a sensitivity check, showing its weight distributions and common support.

Design and precision analysis

Nature Human Behaviour’s guidelines set 0.95 as the a priori power floor for frequentist plans. For inference by Bayes factors, they require either data collection until the Bayes factor reaches 10 in one direction or the other, or a maximum feasible sample at which an inconclusive result would still be an important message for the field. This study uses an estimation design on a census of existing records, so the second provision applies. With no further data to collect, the frozen extract is the maximum feasible sample. The design nonetheless takes its evidence threshold from the first provision. As Decision rules sets out, a non-negligible verdict requires an interval Bayes factor of at least 10 under the reference prior, the weakly informative prior registered under Priors. The assurance level at which a hypothesis counts as adequately powered is registered as 0.90, with every 0.95 requirement reported from the same archived curves (Supplementary Section S5). A hypothesis declared underpowered is still fitted and reported, so its interval bounds the effect even where it cannot decide the hypothesis. The assurance analysis is the fixed-sample counterpart of a Bayes factor design analysis (Schönbrodt & Wagenmakers, 2018).

The text coefficients are identified between distinct texts, so their precision depends on how many the extract contains, a number not yet known. To find how many are needed, we simulated data with pilotr from the deposited design specifications (Bernabeu, 2026b). The texts sweep varied the number of distinct texts at an average of twelve readings per text, the readings sweep varied readings per text at 250 texts and the tier-2 sweep varied the number of children. The simulated tier-1 model contained the three components, the age band, the two age-band interactions and a text intercept. The narration model contained the lexical and continuity components, the narration flag, their interactions with it and a text intercept. The tier-2 model had the three components, a single accumulated-reading trend and child and text intercepts, without the age band or its interactions. In the generated reading times, the text intercept contributed 9 per cent of the variance, the child intercept 20 per cent and the residual the remainder. These shares are assumptions, set before any reading time from this platform could be linked to a text, since no estimate of them exists for this app. The text share matters most for the requirements, and it lies just below published decompositions of children’s reading rates over many passages. Those put the variance between passages at about 10 to 13 per cent (Barth et al., 2014; Francis et al., 2018; Poncy et al., 2005), and at 13 per cent in the logs of a shared-reading app (Z. Wang et al., 2024). In tier 1, each simulated account contributed one reading, so the child variance reached the fitted model as residual variance, and how the remainder divides between child and residual cannot change a simulated tier-1 requirement. The same decompositions put most of the variance between readers, 81 per cent in Poncy et al. (2005). A child share that large would lower the tier-2 requirement, but readings in an unsupervised app are likely to be noisier than assessment readings, so the registered 20 per cent is the cautious choice. Supplementary Section S5 reports the tier-1 requirements re-derived with the text share at 5, 13 and 20 per cent. Over that range, a main effect needed between about 490 and 1,240 distinct texts for a determinate verdict nine times in ten, whereas the interactions needed between about 1,140 and 1,500 and changed little.

Data were simulated on the within-language standardised scale, so a simulated coefficient is directly comparable with the region of practical equivalence. The region’s half-width, 0.05 standard deviations, is a registered convention and the smallest effect this protocol treats as worth reporting. The simulated effect of 0.10 standard deviations, a second registered convention, is twice the half-width, because an effect at the boundary cannot be declared outside the region. A null condition with every coefficient set to zero established how reliably the design can declare practical equivalence and how often it declares a zero effect non-negligible.

The simulated models are simpler than the registered ones, and the simplifications bias the requirements in different directions. They omit the narration terms of the tier-1 model, the reading-level adjustment, the covariates, the language, country and app-version intercepts, the language-varying slopes, the distributional sub-model and the review-derived priors. The simulation generated child variance but analysed a dataset in which each account contributes one reading, so this variance entered the fitted model as inflated residual variance. In that one respect, the requirements below for the reading-time models are upper bounds. In the ordinal model for Hypothesis 5, the unmodelled variance also attenuates every coefficient towards zero, so the projection in Supplementary Section S5 starts from the attenuated estimate and is not an upper bound. Adjusting for reading level, which moves all three feature families together, will remove some of the components’ variance and raise every requirement, whereas the language-varying slopes and the priors will lower them. The prevalence-weighted main effects have the precision of the pooled slope and no design effect, but each interaction incurs the design effect for imbalance applied by the first two adjustments below. When the design analysis is re-run at the observed inputs, we will refit one grid point of each sweep with the registered brms specification, compute both registered criteria from those posteriors and report the difference.

The simulation scored its verdicts by criteria that lie between the registered posterior and Bayes-factor criteria. It counted a verdict as determinate when the 95 per cent interval lay wholly outside the region, and as equivalence when it lay wholly inside. An interval wholly outside the region leaves at most 2.5 per cent of a symmetric posterior inside it, whereas the posterior criterion allows up to 5 per cent. An interval wholly inside the region guarantees at least 95 per cent of the posterior inside it, which is the registered equivalence criterion.

For a non-negligible verdict, the Bayes-factor criterion is stricter than either the posterior criterion or the simulation’s, so it sets the requirement. Under the reference prior, the interval Bayes factor against the region reaches 10 when the posterior mass inside it falls to 0.8 per cent for a main effect and 1.7 per cent for an interaction. For a normal posterior, those masses place the estimate 2.42 and 2.13 standard errors beyond the region’s edge, against the interval’s 1.96. The requirements under this criterion were computed from the same archived curves by that normal approximation, using the deposited script design_analysis/solve_at_bf10.R (checked against the simulation in Supplementary Section S5). The re-run at the observed inputs will compute this criterion by simulation from the brms refits and supersede these approximations.

Pairing the two criteria combines Bayesian estimation with Bayes-factor testing. These approaches differ in whether the model privileges a theoretically useful value such as the null, and each serves a different goal (Rouder et al., 2018). With the same region and prior, though, the interval Bayes factor and the posterior odds of the region come from one posterior and differ only in whether the prior odds are divided out (Campbell & Gustafson, 2023). Calibrated to the same error rate, the interval Bayes factor, the posterior rule and the frequentist equivalence test make almost the same decisions (Campbell & Gustafson, 2021; Linde et al., 2023). The choice between the two criteria therefore turns on which verdict each should decide, and reporting both is the current recommendation for inferences about null and non-null effects (Heck et al., 2023; Lakens et al., 2020). The posterior rule has an independent decision-theoretic justification (Kelter, 2025).

For the three text coefficients (Figure 3, panel a), an effect of 0.10 standard deviations reached a determinate verdict nine times in ten at 738 distinct texts for lexical difficulty (95 per cent interval 640 to 843). Grammatical load needed 660 (593 to 731) and referential continuity 634 (568 to 704). At 0.95, the three coefficients needed 926, 812 and 784. The age-band interactions needed about twice as many: 1,230 texts for grammatical load (1,107 to 1,359) and 1,431 for referential continuity (1,276 to 1,596) at 0.90. At 0.95, the grammatical-load interaction needed 1,510 texts, and the continuity interaction did not reach that target within the 1,600 texts simulated. The narration contrast needed 1,234 texts (1,113 to 1,361) before the design effect for imbalance is applied, and 1,506 at 0.95. That requirement applies to the lexical-by-narration interaction coefficient. It is a lower bound for the difference-of-proportions rule of Hypothesis 4, because that rule’s draw-by-draw ratio has heavier tails than the coefficient it is built from. Declaring practical equivalence when the true effect was zero required a similar number of texts. A main effect was correctly declared equivalent in 87 to 90 per cent of replicates at 800 texts and in every one at 1,600, but the interactions reached 89 to 92 per cent only at 1,600. An inconclusive verdict is therefore predictable from the number of texts alone. A zero main effect or age-band interaction was wrongly declared non-negligible in at most 2 of 120 replicates (1.7 per cent) at every grid point, from 50 to 1,600 texts.

For the unadjusted trend, the within-child term needed 609 children in tier 2 (528 to 697), each reading five distinct texts, and 731 (621 to 851) at 0.95. The registered term is adjusted for the three covariates defined under Measures. Session order and the time since the first session are correlated with accumulated reading within a child, so the unadjusted figure understates the requirement. The tier-2 re-run below re-derives it with all three.

Under the Bayes-factor criterion, the requirements for a non-negligible verdict rise. The three main effects need about 930 distinct texts for lexical difficulty, 930 for grammatical load and 940 for referential continuity at 0.90, and 1,120, 1,120 and 1,130 at 0.95. The grammatical-load interaction needs about 1,470 texts and the continuity interaction 1,480 at 0.90, neither reaching 0.95 within the 1,600 texts simulated. The narration interaction needs about 1,470 texts at 0.90, before the design effect for imbalance, and likewise does not reach 0.95 within 1,600. Tier 2 needs about 810 children at 0.90 and 970 at 0.95.

In the readings sweep, raising readings per text from 2 to 60 (Figure 3, panel b) narrowed the interval on a main effect from 0.19 to 0.08 standard deviations. Even so, a main effect’s probability of a determinate verdict rose no higher than 0.70. Over the same range, that probability for the age-band interactions climbed from under 0.11 to between 0.87 and 0.90. Sessions and texts are therefore not interchangeable. A text coefficient is bounded by how many different texts the extract contains, whereas an interaction between a text property and a reader attribute gains from many different children meeting the same text.

The threshold each hypothesis has to clear is recorded in Table 1 and fixed before any part of the extract is transferred. Seven registered adjustments of the design analysis each draw on the provider-computed counts, the audit of the frozen extract or the archived output of the prior-derivation pipeline. The first two are analytic corrections for imbalance. The simulation balanced the narration contrast, but narration was on in 32 of the 35 sessions of the structural sample. An unbalanced binary contrast at prevalence \(p\) inflates the variance of its interaction coefficient by \(1/(4p(1-p))\) relative to balance. The first adjustment multiplies the simulated Hypothesis 4 requirement by that factor at the observed narration prevalence, about 3.0 at a prevalence of 0.91, and states the adjusted requirement before fitting. The second applies the same factor to the Hypothesis 2 and 3 requirements at the observed age-band prevalence, since the simulation balanced the bands as well. The third re-runs the rating simulation at the rating availability and text reuse in the provider-computed counts. The fourth re-runs it at the observed proportions of the three rating categories. Because the counts report only how often a rating is present, these proportions are tabulated from the transferred extract before fitting, without reference to any text or reader attribute. The values published for an earlier window of this platform (Ratner & Murphy, 2026) cover ages from three years below this study’s younger band, so they are not used as inputs. The fifth re-runs the tier-2 sweep at the observed distribution of sessions per child. For that re-run alone, we will add the three registered covariates to the deposited tier-2 specification, with every term entered as Measures specifies. The simulated sessions will be spaced as in the extract, and story lengths will be generated so that accumulated reading correlates with session order as it does there. The extended specification will be deposited before it is run. The thresholds these three re-runs yield replace the ones in Table 1 for the underpowered declarations of Hypotheses 5 and 6.

The sixth adjustment depends on the output of the prior-derivation pipeline (Supplementary Section S6). Before any outcome datum is accessed, we will record from that output whether the absolute value of each component’s pooled estimate on the model’s scale exceeds 0.10 standard deviations. Where that absolute value lies between 0.05 and 0.10, the texts sweep will be re-simulated with the deposited scripts at that value in place of the 0.10 input. The resulting threshold replaces the one in Table 1 for that component before fitting. If it lies at or below the region’s half-width, no number of texts could yield a determinate verdict at that size. The 0.10 convention is then retained, and the discrepancy will be reported with the component’s verdict. The seventh adjustment runs only if the compression diagnostic falls below its cut-off. It re-runs the texts sweep at the observed number of unnarrated readings per text, taken from the audit, and yields the threshold for the narration rule stated with Hypothesis 1. That threshold is stated before any Hypothesis 1 verdict is taken from an unnarrated slope.

Analysis plan

We will fit all confirmatory models as Bayesian hierarchical models with the brms package and its Stan backend (Bürkner, 2017). Continuous predictors will be standardised within language after transformation, except accumulated reading, whose scaling by within-child variation is set out under Measures. Where the accompanying systematic review supports it, weakly informative priors will be replaced by review-derived priors, as described under Priors.

Outcome-blind dimension reduction

The observed text features are correlated because the reading level a text is written for moves lexical, grammatical and discourse properties together. We will reduce them by principal component analysis, separately within the lexical, grammatical and discourse families, without treating them as effects of an unobserved latent cause (Crossley, 2025). The affective measures will be kept as separate secondary predictors, because valence and arousal are theoretically distinct and need not move together. Narrativity, though computed as a discourse feature, will likewise be excluded from the component solution.

Each distinct text will contribute one row however often it was read, so a popular text does not determine the solution. Within a family, a text enters the solution only if it has valid values on every retained feature. A text with fewer than five sentences, as segmented under Materials, enters no family’s solution. The theory object records this minimum as the boundary condition for referential continuity. Since the tier-1 model includes all three components, a text below the minimum contributes to no confirmatory model, so the thresholds in Table 1 apply to the distinct-text counts that remain. We will report, per language, the distribution of sentences and of words per text and the number of texts the minimum removes.

Counts will be transformed with \(\log(1+x)\) and bounded proportions with an empirical-logit transform where these reduce skew. A feature is dropped from a language if more than 20 per cent of its values are missing or its standard deviation after transformation is below 0.01. The text-coverage rule drops a feature from any language in which fewer than 80 per cent of texts receive a valid token-level match. A language contributes to a family’s solution only if it has at least 100 distinct eligible texts and at least three valid features in that family, two of them anchors (defined below). Where two features correlate at \(|r| > .90\), the retained one is chosen in this order: lower missingness, then broader language coverage, then higher extraction reliability on a manually checked sample, then the measure listed first under Materials. No outcome correlation enters any of these decisions.

To stop the largest language determining the solution, we will compute a correlation matrix within each eligible language, transform the correlations to Fisher’s z and average them with equal weight per language. The average will then be transformed back for analysis (Revelle, 2023). Sampling adequacy requires individual Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin values above .50 and an overall value above .60. Parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) will set the number of components, retaining each component whose eigenvalue exceeds the 95th percentile of the corresponding eigenvalues from 10,000 random datasets. That percentile criterion, Glorfeld’s modification of Horn’s average (Glorfeld, 1995), is the more conservative of the two. The scree plot will be reported but will not override that rule. Where a family retains more than one component, the components will be rotated obliquely with promax, because linguistic properties co-occur and do not vary independently (Biber, 1988). Promax will use \(\kappa\) = 4, the default of the implementation used (Revelle, 2023). We will then apply the regression scoring coefficients from the frozen, language-balanced solution to each text’s features, standardised within language.

The anchors of each confirmatory component are the features that identify it. For LexicalDifficulty, they are frequency, surprisal and word length. Surprisal is grouped with the lexical features because it is hardest to separate from word frequency at the word level (Oh & Schuler, 2025). Model surprisal also diverges most from human reading times on the rarest words (Oh et al., 2024). The correlation between surprisal and each grammatical feature will be reported per language next to the pattern matrix, but the family assignment fixed here does not depend on it. For GrammaticalLoad, the anchors are dependency distance, subordination, clause density, sentence length and noun-phrase depth. For ReferentialContinuity, they are adjacent-sentence similarity, referential overlap and the densities of causal and contrastive connectives. The additive and temporal densities are descriptive only, as stated under Measures, because causal and contrastive connectives raised comprehension, temporal ones had no effect and additive ones lowered it (Kleijn et al., 2019). A count mixing all connective types therefore has no defined expected sign. Each component’s sign is set to match the orientation stated in the Introduction.

Under the construction rule, each confirmatory component must load at least .30 in absolute value on at least two of its anchors. If no component in a family meets this rule, the hypotheses resting on that family are reported as untestable. Where more than one component meets the rule, the one with the most anchors reaching the .30 threshold is confirmatory, and the others are reported descriptively. In the discourse family, a tie is broken in favour of the component on which the causal and contrastive densities load highest, because the cue-utilisation account that Hypothesis 3 adjudicates is stated for connectives. The tie-break, a registered convention, implies no claim that connectives are the more predictive cue, since in the child sample closest to this one, deep and referential cohesion both explained unique variance in comprehension (Pickren et al., 2022). If the component so chosen loads below .30 on both connective densities, Hypothesis 3 is still tested on it, under the narrower interpretation given in Table 1.

Concreteness and age of acquisition come from native norms, which measure them differently in each language. They are therefore not anchors and enter the language-specific solutions but not the pooled one. In a sensitivity analysis, we will add both to the pooled lexical solution, averaging each feature’s correlations over the languages that retain it. We will report that solution with Tucker’s congruence coefficient between its loadings and those of the primary solution. Every congruence coefficient in this protocol will be computed over the features that both solutions contain.

The component solution will be reported in full: the pattern matrix, the component correlations, the variance explained, the language-specific matrices, the loading congruence for the five largest languages and the raw-scale standard deviation of mean dependency distance per language. Language-specific and sparse solutions are sensitivity analyses and will never replace the primary solution after an outcome is seen.

Confirmatory models

In tier 1, we will estimate a model of within-language standardised log reading time per word. Its fixed effects are the three components, the age band, the narration flag, each component’s interactions with the age band and with narration, the reading level, log text length, narrativity, valence, arousal and the generated-or-classic indicator. The age band and narration are coded as deviations from their observed prevalence. The model has varying intercepts for Text, Country and App version, and varying intercepts and uncorrelated component slopes for Language. Under the working assumption stated under Materials, it also has a varying intercept for the underlying story, crossed with Language, which question (d) in Table 2 can remove. Account receives a varying intercept where the Account rule under Sampling plan is met. Hypotheses 1 to 4 will be evaluated in this model. Because the model includes every component-by-age-band and component-by-narration interaction, each registered interaction is adjusted for the others, and each main effect is the prevalence-weighted average slope defined in the Introduction. A component’s slope in a given state is recovered draw by draw as the main effect plus the interaction multiplied by that state’s deviation score. LexicalDifficulty × AgeBand enters no registered test and will not be taken as evidence about whether the age band stands in for a bundle of developing capacities. The three component-by-narration terms enter a registered test only through the state-specific slopes, the lexical and continuity terms through the Hypothesis 4 contrast and all three through the narration rule stated with Hypothesis 1. All four terms will be reported with posterior medians and intervals, so that no term is reported selectively.

The Hypothesis 4 contrast is computed from these state-specific slopes. For each posterior draw, the proportional attenuation of a component is one minus its narrated slope divided by its unnarrated slope, so a positive value means a smaller coefficient under narration. The contrast is computed only under the Hypothesis 4 precondition, which requires fewer than 5 per cent of the draws of each derived unnarrated slope to lie inside the region. The confidence interval for a ratio of two normally distributed estimates is bounded only when its denominator differs significantly from zero (Fieller, 1954; von Luxburg & Franz, 2009). The precondition asks the same of the posterior draws, with the region in place of zero, and makes no claim that either slope is an effect, so the Bayes-factor criterion does not apply. Draws in which either unnarrated slope lies inside the region will be excluded from the ratio, and their fraction reported. As a sensitivity analysis, the contrast will be repeated with a fixed denominator, each narration interaction divided by the posterior median of its unnarrated slope, and reported with the draw-by-draw version.

The first of three further rules for Hypothesis 4 concerns accounts observed in both narration states. If they cover at least as many distinct texts as the adjusted requirement, the within-account contrast restricted to them becomes the primary estimate, and the full-sample contrast is reported with it. If they cover fewer, the within-account contrast remains a sensitivity analysis, and selection into narration state is acknowledged wherever the full-sample estimate is reported.

Second, the tier-1 model contains a distributional sub-model in which the narration flag also predicts the residual standard deviation. The ratio of the narrated to the unnarrated residual standard deviation is the compression diagnostic. A ratio below 0.5 is the registered signature of the audio setting the pace. When the diagnostic falls below that threshold, the full-sample verdict is withheld and Hypothesis 4 is evaluated in the subset described next. For each language, we will report the share of narrated sessions whose rate lies within 10 per cent of that language’s median narrated rate, because the ratio can miss a minority of audio-paced sessions. The share enters no decision rule.

Third, the contrast will be repeated in the narrated sessions the audio cannot have paced. For each language, the narration rate will be estimated as the median words per minute of its narrated sessions, and a narrated session counts as unpaced when its rate exceeds 1.5 times that median. Where a language has fewer than 100 narrated sessions, a fixed bound applies instead. The bound is that language’s adult read-aloud mean in the International Reading Speed Texts or, for a language they do not cover, the slowest of their 17 means, 138 words per minute for Arabic (Trauzettel-Klosinski & Dietz, 2012). The premise is that narration produced for children is paced no faster than adult oral reading in the same language. If Korean has fewer than 100 narrated sessions, the fixed bound classes fewer of its sessions as unpaced, for the reason given under Measures, and the size of this conservative subset will be reported. Pacing does not explain selectivity that persists in the unpaced subset. Hypothesis 4 is reported as underpowered from the subset if the subset contains fewer distinct texts than the adjusted requirement. If the data controller supplies the audio’s duration for every eligible text, or discloses a fixed narration rate, that quantity replaces the median estimate and the fixed bound, and the substitution will be reported. A narrated session then counts as unpaced when its rate exceeds 1.5 times the rate the audio implies. A duration supplied for only some texts defines the unpaced subset among those texts in a sensitivity analysis.

The Hypothesis 5 model will have the same fixed and grouping structure and a cumulative-logit likelihood over the three rating categories, with category thresholds that vary by language. Cross-language differences in use of the middle category are then not mistaken for differences in text difficulty. Whether the Account intercept is estimable in this model depends on how many accounts contribute more than one rated reading, which the tier-1 count does not establish. We will report that number and, where the intercept is not estimable, acknowledge child-level scale use as a limit on the rating. Band-specific odds ratios, derived from the component-by-age-band terms, will be reported descriptively alongside the pooled ones. A text rated by fewer than five distinct children does not contribute, and the primary model is complete-case, with the inverse-probability sensitivity analysis registered under the audit.

For tier 2, we will refit the same specification with a varying intercept for Account and decompose accumulated reading into the account’s mean and the current session’s deviation from it (Curran & Bauer, 2011). Both parts enter with the three covariates set out under Measures, and the deviation term tests Hypothesis 6.

Posterior predictive checks will assess calibration and residual structure by language, age band, reading level and narration state in every model.

Priors

Priors for the main effects of the three components on reading time will be derived from the systematic review by a deterministic pipeline. A derived prior requires at least two independent papers in the review reporting a standardised regression coefficient for the component. The pipeline’s frozen rules translate a pooled estimate to the model’s scale only for reading time and for accuracy. The rating coefficients of Hypothesis 5, on a cumulative-logit scale, and the within-child coefficient of Hypothesis 6 therefore keep weakly informative priors. The pooled estimates the pipeline writes for them will be reported beside their posteriors. The rules were first committed in August 2026 and last revised on 16 September 2026, while the review’s tables of extracted records and effects held no data rows. They are deposited with this protocol, and Supplementary Section S6 summarises them and records the test extractions that preceded them. The pipeline will be run before any outcome datum is accessed. The review’s estimates for the corresponding predictor family and outcome class will be sign-harmonised to the direction in which each component is scored, so that slower reading carries the same sign in every estimate. They will be kept within compatible metric families and pooled by random-effects meta-analysis, with one estimate per paper per relation. The prior is Student’s \(t(3, \hat\mu, \sigma)\), where \(\hat\mu\) is the pooled estimate translated to the model’s scale and \(\sigma\) is twice the combined pooling and between-paper uncertainty, with a floor of 0.10. The doubling and the floor allow for the gap between measures taken in the laboratory and passively timed reading in an app.

The same review motivates the hypotheses, so the evidence that generates a prediction also centres its prior. Two features of the decision rule keep that from being circular. First, the verdict’s posterior criterion is evaluated on the derived-prior fit, but its evidence criterion, the interval Bayes factor defined under Decision rules, is computed from the weakly informative refit described next. A prior centred in the predicted direction therefore does not supply the evidence that confirms the prediction. Second, a hypothesis whose verdict changes under the weakly informative refit is reported as sensitive to the prior, and its result is not counted as conclusive. We will report prior-data conflict diagnostics for every review-derived prior.

Every main effect and interaction without a review-derived prior keeps a weakly informative prior, \(t(3, 0, 0.5)\) for a standardised main effect and \(t(3, 0, 0.25)\) for an interaction. That covers every interaction term, the main effects of narration and the age band, the coefficients of Hypotheses 5 and 6, every secondary predictor and covariate, and any component main effect with fewer than two such papers. Group-level and residual standard deviations take half-\(t(3, 0, 1)\) priors, and any estimated correlation matrix takes LKJ(\(\eta\) = 2). Intercept priors will be calibrated by prior-predictive simulation to the outcome scale, without reference to any predictor-outcome association. The full prior table and its forest plots will be archived before the extract is transferred and will accompany the Stage 2 report. If the review’s extraction has not produced the table within the four weeks expected under Timeline, the transfer will wait for it for up to three months after in-principle acceptance. If the table is still missing then, the weakly informative priors will be primary and the pipeline will not be run, and either the delay or the change will be reported. We will refit every model with weakly informative priors throughout and with the derived scales halved and doubled. Where a meta-analysis in the review subsumes other papers in it, every model whose priors it informs will also be refitted with priors from the alternative pooling described in Supplementary Section S6. Conclusions will be reported under each refit.

Decision rules and diagnostics

For every confirmatory coefficient, we will report the posterior median, the 95 per cent highest-density interval, the probability of the predicted direction, the posterior proportion inside the region of practical equivalence and a model-implied contrast on an interpretable scale. The region is \(|\beta| < 0.05\) standard deviations for a one-standard-deviation change in a predictor of reading time, and a half-width of log(1.05) on the log-odds scale for the rating model. More than 95 per cent of the posterior inside the region counts as practical equivalence. A practically non-negligible association is labelled in the predicted or opposite direction by the sign of the posterior median. It requires less than 5 per cent of the posterior inside the region and an interval Bayes factor of at least 10 for the region on that side against the region inside. Any other result is inconclusive.

The Bayes factor is the ratio of the posterior odds of the two regions to their prior odds (Hoijtink et al., 2019; Klugkist et al., 2005; Morey & Rouder, 2011). The posterior odds come from the draws of the weakly informative refit, whose Student-t prior is the reference prior for evidence. The prior odds come from that prior’s mass in the region, known in closed form. No marginal likelihood is needed, so the Bayes factor avoids the fragility that marginal-likelihood Bayes factors show in mixed models (Schad & Modrák, 2026; van Doorn et al., 2023). For the two-sided interactions of Hypotheses 2 and 3, it is computed for each side against the region, and the verdict states the side the evidence favours. Every Bayes factor is reported alongside its verdict, with its values under the derived priors and their halved and doubled scales, from the refits registered under Priors.

Which of the two criteria is stricter depends on the kind of verdict. Under the reference prior, 95 per cent of the posterior outside the region corresponds to a Bayes factor of only 1.5 for a main effect and 3.2 for an interaction. These values are low because the prior already places most of its mass outside the region, so the Bayes factor decides non-negligibility. For equivalence, 95 per cent inside corresponds to a Bayes factor above 100 for the region. A Bayes factor of 10 alone, by contrast, would be reached with less than half of the posterior inside it, so the posterior mass decides equivalence (Kruschke, 2018). Taking the stricter criterion for each kind of verdict keeps the journal’s evidence threshold for Bayes factors without letting a diffuse prior produce an equivalence verdict.

The same two criteria apply to the Hypothesis 5 odds ratios on the log-odds scale. For the Hypothesis 5 ordering clause, a posterior probability of at least 0.90 that the three coefficients rank as they do for reading time counts as agreement on order, and anything lower as order not established. The Hypothesis 4 contrast, a derived quantity, is judged by three posterior criteria. They are at least 95 per cent of the posterior of the difference of attenuations above 0.20, more than 95 per cent inside the region and more than 95 per cent below -0.05. Table 1 gives the interpretation of each outcome, and a posterior meeting none of the criteria is inconclusive. We will report the interval Bayes factors of the contrast’s two constituent narration interactions. The fixed-denominator sensitivity contrast is evaluated against the same thresholds.

Each hypothesis is a single registered test with its own decision rule, and no claim aggregates evidence across hypotheses, so no correction for multiple comparisons will be applied. The one composite claim, the combined Hypothesis 1 verdict, follows an intersection-union rule. It is recorded only when all three coefficients are individually non-negligible in the predicted direction, so its error rate cannot exceed that of any single component. Hierarchical shrinkage and the null condition of the design analysis, which reports how often a zero effect is wrongly declared non-negligible, calibrate the per-decision error rates behind Table 1.

Six registered contingencies depend on quantities computed after fitting, each a conditional rule stated where it applies. Two can leave a hypothesis without a verdict. Failure of the Hypothesis 4 precondition leaves Hypothesis 4 not evaluable, and failure of the positive control on recorded reading time leaves Hypotheses 1 to 4 and 6 not evaluable. Of the other four, the compression diagnostic, when it indicates compression, withholds the full-sample Hypothesis 4 verdict in favour of the unpaced subset. Under the narration rule stated with Hypothesis 1, the same diagnostic can move a component’s Hypothesis 1 verdict to its derived unnarrated slope. Draws with an unnarrated slope inside the region are excluded from the Hypothesis 4 ratio. The third outcome-neutral check (next section) makes the within-level estimate primary for a component whose within-level coefficient falls below half its pooled magnitude. The prior-sensitivity rule above applies when a verdict changes under the weakly informative refit. Two further rules respond to the fitting itself and not to any estimated association. Models failing the sampling diagnostics are simplified in the order given below, and a failed permutation check withholds the confirmatory results until the source of the structure is found and reported.

We will run four chains of 4,000 iterations, half of them warm-up. Acceptable sampling requires \(\hat{R}\) below 1.01, bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 1,000 for every confirmatory parameter, no divergent transitions, satisfactory energy diagnostics and no unresolved maximum-treedepth warnings. Models failing these criteria will be refitted with a higher target acceptance rate, greater tree depth and more iterations. If simplification is still needed, every estimable varying intercept will be retained, and the language-level slopes will be removed in the fixed order continuity, then lexical, then grammatical. The model already omits the correlations among these slopes, the first parameters to drop when a random-effects structure is simplified (Barr et al., 2013; Matuschek et al., 2017). The order follows the Introduction, which gives no reason for the continuity slope to vary across languages and expects the grammatical slope to vary most. Retaining that slope longest accords with advice to keep components expected on theoretical grounds (Matuschek et al., 2017). We will report every simplification and its diagnostic consequence. Variance inflation factors will be reported for the fixed-effect design matrix, but a high value will not prompt an outcome-dependent deletion. The joint model remains primary, and single-family models will be reported as a collinearity sensitivity analysis.

For Hypotheses 2, 3 and 4, we will compare the model with one omitting the relevant interaction, using Pareto-smoothed importance-sampling leave-one-out cross-validation. We will report the expected log pointwise predictive density difference, its standard error and the Pareto-\(k\) diagnostics, with exact refits where importance sampling is unreliable. Predictive comparison supplements the coefficient-level summaries and decides no verdict.

Outcome-neutral quality checks

Six checks are registered, each with a pass criterion and a consequence of failure. All but the fifth run before the confirmatory models are interpreted. The fifth, convergent validity, is the ordering clause of Hypothesis 5 and is evaluated with that hypothesis, which states the check’s outcome if the hypothesis is underpowered. The first, second, fourth and sixth are decided without reference to any confirmatory coefficient, whereas the third is a robustness condition on the fitted text coefficients.

For the first check, a permutation null, we will reassign texts at random to readings within language and reading level, and refit the tier-1 model 200 times. The refits will use the maximum-likelihood analogue of that model, with the same fixed and estimable grouping structure. We choose this approximation because 200 full Bayesian refits are not feasible and the check concerns structure introduced by the pipeline. With no posterior or Bayes factor to evaluate, each refit is scored by the design analysis’s criterion for a determinate verdict, a 95 per cent Wald interval wholly outside the region. No text coefficient may meet that criterion in more than 5 per cent of the refits. An inconclusive verdict on permuted data is expected at moderate text counts and is not a failure. Failure would mean the pipeline has introduced structure that does not come from the pairing of a text with a reading. In that case, we will withhold the confirmatory results until the source of that structure is found and reported.

The second check, a positive control on recorded reading time, requires total elapsed time to rise with the story’s word count, with more than 99 per cent of the posterior above zero. Failure means recorded reading time does not index reading, and Hypotheses 1 to 4 and 6 are reported as not evaluable on this extract.

The third check asks whether the association is an artefact of platform assignment. We will re-estimate the three text coefficients separately within each reading level, and each must retain at least half its pooled magnitude. Where a coefficient falls short, the within-level estimate becomes primary for that component. The pooled estimate is then reported alongside it, and the assignment confound is named wherever the coefficient appears.

The fourth check tests three independence implications of the theory object. First, conditional on the reading level and the generated-or-classic indicator, the three components should be close to independent across texts. Second, narration should be unrelated to accumulated reading. Third, the difficulty rating should carry no further information about reading level once reading time is conditioned on. We will report each as an estimate with an interval. An implication fails when the 95 per cent interval of its conditional association lies entirely beyond 0.10 in standardised units. Such a failure does not invalidate the confirmatory tests and is reported as showing a path the causal graph omits. For the third implication, the omitted path would run directly to the reported difficulty from the reader’s capacity, which the reading level partly indexes, or from the generation route. That path would be stated with the Hypothesis 5 odds ratios. Supplementary Section S1 gives the literature’s reasons to expect this implication to fail, but the same criterion applies.

The sixth check validates the feature pipeline by hand. In a stratified random sample of 50 texts per language, we will inspect tokenisation, sentence splitting and dependency attachment, and report agreement per language before any feature is linked to outcome data. A native speaker of each language will adjudicate the sentence boundaries in 20 of these texts, and we will report how far the automatic sentence splits agree with that adjudication. A language whose labelled-attachment agreement with the hand-checked parse falls below .85 contributes to descriptive reporting but not to the grammatical component.

Sensitivity analyses

Eight families of sensitivity analysis are registered here, all of them descriptive. Each bounds a primary estimate without replacing it, and none changes which hypothesis is tested or how its verdict is reached. Others are specified where they apply, among them the resource contrasts in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 and the two refits registered with Hypothesis 6.

In the first family, we will refit the reading-time models with no plausibility bounds, with winsorisation at the 1st and 99th and at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles within language, and with posterior-predictive residual trimming. Where the censoring rule applies, we will report the censored and uncensored fits side by side. The tier-1 model will be refitted with the outcome per character, counted in the unit Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 use for word length (jamo in Korean, characters in Chinese and consonant and vowel letters in Thai), and its three component coefficients reported with the primary estimates.

The second family redefines eligibility, moving the tier-2 threshold from three distinct texts to two and to five and, as a practice analysis, adding repeated readings with a repetition indicator. With the sentence floor raised to eight, we will refit the tier-1 model and report its three component coefficients and distinct-text count with the primary estimates. Grouping by Text will be compared with grouping on the attribute combination that generated the text. The classic stories are likely to be public-domain texts, which the surprisal model’s training corpus could contain (Supplementary Section S3). We will therefore report the mean per-word surprisal of classic and of generated texts per language before any outcome data are linked. For the same reason, we will refit the tier-1 model on generated texts alone, reporting its three component coefficients with the primary estimates.

The third family examines variation between languages, beginning with Hypothesis 6. Where Language, within tier 2, meets the grouping-factor rule under Measures, we will refit the tier-2 model with an uncorrelated language-varying slope on the accumulated-reading deviation and report the per-language slopes. If that refit fails the sampling diagnostics under Decision rules, it will be dropped and the drop reported. We will restrict analyses to the five largest languages and fit language-specific models and component solutions, reporting their loading congruence. From those fits, we will report the two age-band interactions per language next to the pooled coefficients, arranged by the orthographic depth assigned with Hypothesis 6. A further tier-1 refit will let both interactions vary by Language. The design analysis computed no requirement for the language-specific interactions or the language-varying refit, and both will give wide per-language posteriors. Neither supplies a second test of Hypothesis 2 or 3. The simplification order does not remove the language-varying interaction slopes, because they lie outside the tier-1 specification.

The fourth family asks how much the estimates depend on who is reading in which language. Before the transfer, we will deposit a lookup table marking whether each reading language is an official or majority language of each country, as a coarse indicator of the match between the two. We will report the cross-tabulation of reading language by account country and the number of accounts reading in more than one language. We will then refit the tier-1 model on sessions whose reading language is a language of the account’s country. Its three component coefficients will be reported with the full-sample estimates, and its distinct-text count with the threshold in Table 1. The indicator enters no confirmatory model. Because it misclassifies in both directions, the refit bounds the estimates’ sensitivity to sample composition and identifies no individual reader.

The fifth family rescores the text properties, replacing the component scores with sparse-component scores and with pre-specified equal-weight composites of the anchor features. For Hypothesis 3, we will report the age-band interaction of each ReferentialContinuity anchor from a model in which that anchor replaces the component, to check whether the four anchors agree in sign.

The sixth family varies the age contrast in Hypotheses 2 and 3. Where the audit finds the age field finer than two bands, we will re-estimate both age-band interactions with the finer age entered in two ways and report each coding’s estimates with the two-band contrast. One coding treats the finer age as an ordinal monotonic predictor (Bürkner & Charpentier, 2020), the other as an unordered factor contrasting each finer band with the band for ages 7 to 8. Pages per hundred words, computed from the recorded page count, will enter a tier-1 refit along with its age-band interaction. The Hypothesis 2 and 3 interactions from that fit will be reported with the primary ones, to gauge how much of the age difference is time away from the text.

The seventh family adds analyses for Hypotheses 4 and 5. For narration, we will report the within-account contrast, where it is not already primary and can be formed, and the fixed-denominator contrast registered under Confirmatory models. We will also report the Hypothesis 4 contrast within each age band and the duration-adjusted contrast from a refit adding the spoken duration per word and its interaction with narration. Materials states when that duration is measured and when it is predicted. For the rating, we will compare the model under adjacent-category and cumulative-logit links, stratify it by language and set the inverse-probability analysis for rating non-response against the complete-case estimates.

Finally, the eighth family applies alternative regions of practical equivalence: 0.03 and 0.10 standard deviations for reading time, and half-widths of log(1.03) and log(1.10) on the log-odds scale for the rating. Interval Bayes factors will be recomputed for each region.

To ease comparison with earlier studies, we will report maximum-likelihood mixed models with the same fixed and estimable grouping structure in a supplementary table. They are descriptive and will decide no Bayesian verdict.

Contingent hypotheses

Questions (j) and (m) bear on four further hypotheses. We specify them in full but outside the confirmatory set, because each depends on facts about the platform or the extract that have not yet been established.

A contingent hypothesis is activated only if its condition is established before submission. Facts about the platform require the data controller’s written confirmation. The requirement that accounts be observed in both narration states is met if the provider-computed counts show any account that switched narration and the revised count script finds no account identifier in floating-point notation. Any record activating a hypothesis would be reproduced in Supplementary Section S7. At submission, no hypothesis has its full condition established, so none of the four is activated, as that section records. If a condition is established after submission, its hypothesis is reported at Stage 2 under Exploratory analyses and is not counted among the confirmatory tests. We retain the four hypotheses so that any such analysis is the one written before we had any access to the extract’s outcome data. A hypothesis whose condition is never established is not run.

C1: coherence and inferential comprehension. Its condition is that item-level comprehension responses exist and include a recoverable question type. In an item-level model of response correctness, with inference questions coded 1 and literal questions 0, we would predict a positive ReferentialContinuity × QuestionType interaction. The association of continuity with correctness would then be larger where the question requires integration. We found no study that tests a continuity by question-type interaction directly. The prediction instead combines three results: an inference question is answered by integrating information across clauses (Freed & Cain, 2021), cohesion predicts children’s comprehension overall (Pickren et al., 2022), and text properties bear differently on surface and inferential questions (Aparicio et al., 2022).

C2: narration and comprehension. Its condition is that item-level comprehension responses exist and that accounts are observed both with and without narration. We would predict that, within an account, narration is positively associated with response correctness after adjustment for text, age band, reading level, accumulated reading and language. The expected size is small (Clinton-Lisell, 2023; Wood et al., 2018), and the coefficient identifies an association, not an effect.

C3: reading pace and comprehension. It has the same condition as C2 and is restricted to sessions without narration. We would predict that an account’s mean log reading time per word is negatively associated with response correctness, so that accounts with a faster habitual pace answer more accurately (Sortkær et al., 2021). The within-account deviation term in the same model separates this between-account association from slow reading in a difficult or interrupted session.

C4: machine-generated and human-authored text. Its condition is that the classic stories are confirmed to be fixed, human-authored texts, identical for all users within a language. We would predict that the three text components have coefficients of the same sign for reading pace in generated and classic texts. Each component’s interaction with the generated-or-classic indicator is predicted to be practically equivalent to zero. The hypothesis tests whether the two kinds of text are exchangeable. The closest evidence is the adult eye-movement corpus on machine-generated text (Bolliger et al., 2025). A practically non-negligible interaction would mean that a text property relates differently to children’s reading pace in generated and human-authored stories.
